# Chan Traditions Beyond Huineng's Southern School
### A Comprehensive Study of the Bodhidharma Lineage's Other Branches and Meditation Systems Outside It

## Introduction: Scope, Framework, and Methodology

"Chan" (禪) is a transliteration of the Sanskrit *dhyāna* (禪那), corresponding to the Pali *jhāna*, meaning "quiet contemplation" or "cultivation through reflection." It denotes a family of one-pointed concentration practices widespread across Indian śramaṇa traditions—particularly yogic lineages—later absorbed into Buddhism as the core of the "training in concentration" (定學) within the Threefold Training (śīla-samādhi-prajñā, 戒定慧). It appears broadly in contexts ranging from the seventh limb of the Yoga Sutra's eightfold path to the "four dhyānas and eight samādhis" (四禪八定) of early Buddhism. In its broadest sense, then, "Chan" extends far beyond the sectarian "Chan School" that Chinese Buddhist history traces from Bodhidharma (菩提達摩) as founding patriarch, through Huike (慧可), Sengcan (僧璨), Daoxin (道信), and Hongren (弘忍), to Huineng (惠能)—whose "special transmission outside the teachings" then branched into the Five Houses and Seven Schools (Linji, Caodong, Guiyang, Yunmen, Fayan), and which subsequently spread to the Korean peninsula (the Jogye Order and its branches), Japan (Rinzai, Sōtō, Ōbaku), and Vietnam (Liễu Quán, Trúc Lâm, and Lâm Tế lineages).

This study takes that distinction as its point of departure: it systematically surveys every substantively independent "Chan" or meditation system within Buddhism—and within Chinese indigenous thought insofar as it interacted closely with Buddhism—that falls outside this sectarian lineage marked by Bodhidharma as its emblematic founder. This includes early Chinese meditation traditions that predate Bodhidharma and that, for a considerable period, carried more social influence than the Bodhidharma lineage itself; it includes the Tiantai (天台) contemplative system, Pure Land meditative practice, and Yogācāra contemplation, each of which developed along an independent lineage contemporaneous with the Bodhidharma school; it includes the profound contemplative systems of both the Chinese and Tibetan branches of Esoteric Buddhism; and it includes the Theravāda śamatha-vipassanā tradition, preserved intact to this day, which sparked a worldwide revival movement in the twentieth century. Finally, in the interest of genuine comprehensiveness, this study will also briefly address two traditions that are not Buddhist per se but that interacted deeply with Buddhist meditation and are sometimes loosely grouped with it under the rubric of "quiet sitting"—the Confucian (Song-Ming Neo-Confucian) quiet-sitting tradition and the Daoist tradition of stilling the mind—and will conclude with a brief account of the origins of the modern, secularized "mindfulness" movement.

Three methodological points require advance clarification:

**First, the stratification of source material.** In the history of Chinese Buddhism, sectarian transmission histories (such as the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* 景德傳燈錄 and *Chuanfa Zhengzong Ji* 傳法正宗記) frequently diverge from—and sometimes flatly contradict—the more historiographical genre of monk biographies (such as the *Gaoseng Zhuan* 高僧傳, *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* 續高僧傳, and *Song Gaoseng Zhuan* 宋高僧傳) on the same figures and events; the discovery of the Dunhuang manuscripts in the early twentieth century further destabilized the reliability of many traditional "orthodox" narratives at their foundations. This study relies, wherever possible, on earlier and more credible sources (Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*, for instance, is generally preferred over the later transmission-record literature), and explicitly flags the degree of certainty and the state of scholarly disagreement at key junctures, rather than asserting unfounded certainty.

**Second, the parallel treatment of insider and academic perspectives.** For each meditation system examined, this study attempts both to present its own internal doctrinal self-understanding (such as the Tiantai school's own view of its dharma-transmission, or the Nyingma school's own account of Dzogchen's provenance) and to bring modern academic Buddhological scholarship—particularly the philological and historical findings of the twentieth century onward—to bear on, test, and where necessary revise, that self-understanding. The two are placed side by side rather than having one simply cancel out the other.

**Third, an important mid-course correction to the scope.** Defining the excluded zone as the entirety of the "Bodhidharma-lineage Chan School" (i.e., the complete lineage running from Bodhidharma through the Five Houses and Seven Schools) would cast too wide a net: it would exclude the original teachings of the "shared patriarchs" themselves—Bodhidharma, Huike, Sengcan, Daoxin, and Hongren—along with Shenxiu's (神秀) "Northern School," which at one point in the High Tang eclipsed the Southern School in prominence; the independently-developing Oxhead School (Niutou School, 牛頭宗); and a series of early meditation lineages—including the Jingzhong (淨眾) and Baotang (保唐) schools of Sichuan—that were gradually marginalized, and eventually written out of the mainstream historical record altogether, in the course of the later "special transmission outside the teachings" orthodox narrative taking shape. These are precisely the branches richest in historical tension within the Bodhidharma lineage, and the ones that most vividly illustrate how "orthodoxy" and "mainstream" status come to be historically constructed—they ought to be a central focus of this study, not a blind spot.

This study therefore defines the excluded zone precisely as: the lineage vigorously championed by Heze Shenhui (荷澤神會), codified through the *Platform Sūtra* (壇經), which takes Huineng as the "Sixth Patriarch" and subsequently branches into the Five Houses and Seven Schools (Linji, Caodong, Guiyang, Yunmen, Fayan), and which spread eastward to the Korean peninsula (the various branches of the Jogye Order), Japan (Rinzai, Sōtō, and Ōbaku), and Vietnam (the Liễu Quán, Trúc Lâm, and Lâm Tế lineages)—in short, **"Huineng's Southern School."** This is the branch that ultimately "won" the historical contest and survives as the mainstream lineage today. Every "Chan" or meditation system that does not identify with this Huineng–Southern School lineage, or that developed historically independent of it, falls within the scope of this study. This means that the shared early teachings from Bodhidharma through Hongren, the Northern School under Shenxiu, the Oxhead School, and the Sichuan lineages are now included as central chapters in their own right, rather than being relegated to boundary-case footnotes.

The study is organized into fifteen parts: (1) early Chinese translations of meditation texts predating Bodhidharma; (2) the Sengchou (僧稠) lineage, the dominant Northern Dynasties meditation tradition developing in parallel with the Bodhidharma school; (3) Bodhidharma through Hongren: the shared early teachings and the formation of the "East Mountain Teaching"; (4) the Northern School under Shenxiu and the rise and fall of its historical standing; (5) the Oxhead School and Farong's (法融) Prajñā-based Chan; (6) the Sichuan lineages—the Jingzhong and Baotang schools—and their transmission into Tibet; (7) the Tiantai contemplative system; (8) Pure Land meditation; (9) Yogācāra contemplation; (10) the Huayan (華嚴) Dharmadhātu contemplation; (11) Chinese Esoteric Buddhist meditation; (12) Tibetan Esoteric meditation (Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen); (13) Theravāda śamatha-vipassanā and the twentieth-century vipassanā revival; (14) a comparative look at indigenous Chinese traditions of quiet contemplation; and (15) the modern, secularized mindfulness movement. A conclusion, suggestions for further research, and a select bibliography follow.

## Part One: Before Bodhidharma—Early Meditation Texts in Chinese Translation (c. 2nd–early 5th Century)

Bodhidharma's own birth and death dates, and even the date of his arrival in China, remain unsettled (scholarly opinion generally places his period of activity in the late fifth to early sixth century, i.e., the transition from the Liu Song to the Liang dynasty). Yet Chinese Buddhist history had already possessed, for some three centuries before this, a clearly documented tradition of translating and transmitting meditation texts. This tradition not only predates Bodhidharma; its influence continued to permeate the Bodhidharma lineage and nearly every subsequent tradition of Chinese Buddhist meditation. In other words, the "meditation sūtra" (禪經) tradition forms the very foundation on which the entire edifice of Chinese Buddhist contemplative culture was built.

### 1. An Shigao's Lineage: Sarvāstivādin Meditation and "Mindfulness of Breathing"

The first translator to systematically render meditation texts into Chinese was An Shigao (安世高), a monk from Parthia (Anxi 安息, in present-day northeastern Iran) who reached Luoyang in the second year of the Jianhe reign of Emperor Huan of the Eastern Han (148 CE). According to the *Gaoseng Zhuan* compiled by Huijiao of the Liang dynasty, An Shigao was "thoroughly versed in the scriptural canon, especially expert in Abhidharma studies, and recited the meditation sūtras, having mastered their subtleties to the utmost." Over more than twenty years in China he translated upward of thirty works; among those directly concerned with meditation, the most important is the *Scripture on the Mindfulness of In-and-Out Breathing* (Foshuo Da Anban Shouyi Jing 佛說大安般守意經, two fascicles), alongside the *Yinchiru Jing* (陰持入經), the *Chanxing Faxiang Jing* (禪行法想經), and the *Daodi Jing* (道地經, an abridged translation from Saṅgharakṣa's *Yogācārabhūmi*, i.e. the *Xiuxing Daodi Jing*).

"Ānāpāna-smṛti" (安般守意) is a compound transliteration-cum-translation of the Sanskrit *ānāpānasmṛti* ("mindfulness of in- and out-breath"): "ana" refers to inhalation, "pana" to exhalation, and "shouyi" (守意, "guarding the mind") renders *smṛti*, mindfulness that holds the scattered mind in check. The meditation method this scripture describes takes the observation of the breath as its point of entry, unfolding through the "six wondrous gates" (六妙門) of counting, following, stopping, contemplating, returning, and purifying, in combination with the contemplation of impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, and the analysis of the elements (which, together with breath-counting, make up the "five methods for stilling the mind," 五停心觀) as antidotes to craving, aversion, delusion, scattered discursive thought, and mixed afflictions. Doctrinally it belongs to the Sarvāstivādin (說一切有部) system, emphasizing the fine-grained analysis of mental factors characteristic of Abhidharma—a methodology strikingly different from the later Bodhidharma lineage's approach of "directly pointing to the mind."

The textual history of the *Anban Shouyi Jing* is itself a noteworthy case study in philology: Kang Senghui of the Wu kingdom during the Three Kingdoms period wrote a commentary on it (only his preface survives today), and Zhi Mindu, Xie Fu, Zhi Dun, and Dao'an of the Eastern Jin all subsequently wrote sub-commentaries, though all of these have since been lost. Twentieth-century scholarship at one point held that the transmitted *Da Anban Shouyi Jing* was no longer An Shigao's original translation but rather a later "expanded edition" in which translated text and commentary had become intermingled; in 1999 the Japanese scholar Ochiai Toshinori discovered, at Kongō-ji temple on Mount Amano in Osaka Prefecture, an older manuscript of a "(Lesser) Anban Shouyi Jing" that more closely approximates the original translation, providing new material for sorting out the textual strata of this scripture; related fragments also survive among the Dunhuang manuscripts (e.g., S.4221). This discovery history in itself suggests that research on the early meditation sūtras remains heavily dependent on the ongoing progress of manuscript philology, and that many conclusions remain provisional.

### 2. Kumārajīva's Lineage: The Five Gates of Chan and the Early Synthesis of Mahāyāna Meditation Essentials

Kumārajīva (鳩摩羅什) reached Chang'an in the third year of the Hongshi reign of the Later Qin (401 CE). Over the following decade or so, alongside translating the *Larger Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra*, the *Lotus Sūtra*, the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, the *Śata-śāstra*, and the *Dvādaśanikāya-śāstra*—works of the Prajñāpāramitā and Madhyamaka corpus—he also systematically translated a body of meditation texts, chief among them:

- The *Zuochan Sanmei Jing* (坐禪三昧經, also known as the *Chan Jing*, *Bodhisattva Chanfa Jing*, or *Chanfa Yao*; translated in the fourth year of Hongshi, 402 CE): this text was not composed by Kumārajīva himself but is a compilation drawing on the meditation teachings of seven masters—Vasumitra, Saṅgharakṣa, Upagupta, Saṅghasena, the Bhikṣu Le, Aśvaghoṣa, and Kumāralāta—hence its alternative name, the "Guanzhong Chan Jing" (關中禪經, "Meditation Scripture of the Guanzhong Region"), a designation that pairs it with the "Lushan Chan Jing" (廬山禪經, "Meditation Scripture of Mount Lu") translated somewhat later by Buddhabhadra. The upper fascicle sets out in detail the "five gates of Chan" (五門禪法) for counteracting the mental states of craving, aversion, delusion, scattered discursive thought, and evenly-mixed afflictions: contemplation of impurity, loving-kindness contemplation, contemplation of dependent origination, mindfulness of breathing (ānāpāna), and buddha-recollection contemplation, each corresponding to one of the five root afflictive tendencies; the lower fascicle goes on to treat the four dhyānas, the four formless attainments, the five supernormal powers, the four foundations of mindfulness, and other śrāvaka-vehicle contemplative stages, concluding with the bodhisattva's five gates and the three forbearances, capped by twenty verses attributed to Aśvaghoṣa—the whole combining Sarvāstivādin and Mahāyāna perspectives.
- The *Chanfa Yaojie* (禪法要解, two fascicles, translated in stages between the fourth and fourteenth years of Hongshi, also known as the *Chanyao Jing*): rather than a line-by-line commentary, this text offers a summary exposition of essential meditation methods, covering the contemplations of purity and impurity, the four dhyānas, the four immeasurables, the four formless attainments, contemplation of the four noble truths, and the practices of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; it is preserved in fascicle fifteen of the Taishō canon.
- Also from this period, per the catalogue records of the *Chu Sanzang Ji Ji* and the *Lidai Sanbao Ji*, are the *Siwei Lüeyao Fa* (思惟略要法) and the *Chan Miyao Jing* (禪祕要經), part of the same systematic effort to propagate meditation methods in this era.

Sengrui, a disciple of Kumārajīva, recalled in his *Preface to the Meditation Scriptures Issued from Guanzhong* that before Kumārajīva's arrival in the East, the meditation literature circulating in China "though covering the subject matter, lacked thorough grounding and offered no method of reception"—that is, it lacked a complete curriculum of practice and a basis of transmission—and that only once Kumārajīva brought the essential teachings of the various masters did "Chan method" acquire a relatively systematic doctrinal and methodological foundation in China. It is worth noting that this body of meditation sūtras was itself a compiled synthesis of the practical experience of multiple Indian and Central Asian meditation masters (Saṅgharakṣa and others), rather than the insight of any single originating figure—a "drawing together of the strengths of many houses" that stands in sharp contrast to the later Chan narrative's emphasis on a single unbroken lineage of "mind-to-mind transmission."

### 3. The "Dharmatrāta Meditation Scripture": A Paradigmatic Case of Historical Name-Confusion

Within the genealogy of early meditation scriptures, one text's very title constitutes a case study worth examining closely in its own right—one with real methodological bearing on how we should understand the relationship between the name "Damo" (達摩, "Dharma[trāta/Bodhidharma]") and the meditation traditions that predate Bodhidharma.

This scripture's original title was the *Xiuxing Fangbian Chan Jing* (修行方便禪經, also known as the *Chan Jing Xiuxing Fangbian* or *Xiuxing Di Bujing Guan Jing*). It was translated at Mount Lu by Buddhabhadra (佛陀跋陀羅, a descendant of the Śākya clan of Kapilavastu), at the request of Huiyuan (慧遠) of Mount Lu, between the seventh and eighth years of the Yixi reign of the Eastern Jin (411–412 CE). Its content transmits the gradual Sarvāstivādin-lineage meditation methods of the Kashmiri meditation master Buddhasena (佛大先, also rendered Buddhasena); paired with the "Guanzhong Chan Jing" (the *Zuochan Sanmei Jing*), it accordingly became known as the "Lushan Chan Jing."

In later catalogues, however, this scripture came to be mistitled the *Damoduoluo Chan Jing* (達摩多羅禪經, "Dharmatrāta Meditation Scripture"), and has circulated under that name in every edition of the canon down to the present. The scripture's preface, in tracing its lineage of transmission, does indeed mention a certain "Venerable Dharmatrāta" (尊者達摩多羅)—but this Dharmatrāta was a teacher within the Kashmiri lineage of meditation transmission, positioned before Buddhasena, an ābhidharmika-cum-meditation-master closely associated with Sarvāstivādin scholasticism. His exact dates are difficult to establish with certainty, but by the reckoning of generational succession he must considerably predate Buddhabhadra's translation work in the early fifth century, and still more so the "Bodhidharma" (菩提達摩) who arrived in China at the very end of the fifth or beginning of the sixth century. The two figures share only the coincidence of an abbreviated Chinese rendering—"Damo" (達摩) serving as shorthand for both "Damoduoluo/Dharmatrāta" and "Putidamo/Bodhidharma"—and were conflated by later copyists and cataloguers unfamiliar with the underlying details. Modern scholarship (Tang Yongtong and others have already offered correctives here) has by now largely clarified that this scripture bears no actual lineage or textual relationship to the founding patriarch of the Chan School who came later; the title "Dharmatrāta Meditation Scripture" is, in fact, a historical mistitling that has persisted for over a millennium.

This case deserves particular emphasis, not only because it bears directly on the disambiguating task of this study, but because it alerts us to something more general: the phenomenon of "accretion through layered transmission" in the copying and cataloguing of Chinese Buddhist texts occurs within the field of meditation literature just as it does in the construction of sectarian transmission genealogies. Intriguingly, modern scholars have also noted that this scripture's popularity declined after the Sui-Tang period—partly because Zhiyi's *Mohe Zhiguan* and other Tiantai contemplative works, together with the rise of the Chan school's own "master-to-disciple" pedagogical style, drew practitioners' attention elsewhere, and partly because the scripture's extensive use of older Sarvāstivādin Abhidharma terminology made it difficult reading for audiences from the Sui-Tang period onward—only regaining scholarly and religious attention in the modern era.

### 4. Dao'an, Huiyuan, and the Meditation-and-Wisdom Tradition of Mount Lu

Dao'an (道安, 312–385) of the Eastern Jin is best known as a critic of the "matching concepts" (geyi 格義) approach to Prajñāpāramitā studies, as the compiler of a scriptural catalogue (the *Zongli Zhongjing Mulu*), and as a founder of monastic institutional practice (including the standardization of "Shi" 釋 as the surname for all monastics). Yet he also wrote commentaries—now mostly lost—on the meditation scriptures translated by An Shigao, showing that contemplative practice held a real place even within this heavily "doctrinal" monastic community.

Dao'an's disciple Huiyuan (慧遠, 334–416) carried this legacy forward: in the fifteenth year of the Taiyuan reign of the Eastern Jin (390 CE, by some accounts slightly earlier), he and 123 monastic and lay followers, including Liu Yimin, formed the "White Lotus Society" at Mount Lu, taking a vow before an image of Amitābha Buddha to jointly cultivate buddha-recollection and together aspire to rebirth in the Pure Land—the earliest clearly documented instance of a communal buddha-recollection society in Chinese Buddhist history, on the strength of which later Pure Land tradition retrospectively honored Huiyuan as its "first patriarch." It is particularly worth emphasizing that the primary scripture on which Huiyuan relied was the *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra* (般舟三昧經) rather than the vocal-recitation-based system that would later become prevalent; his practice centered on "visualization buddha-recollection" (觀想念佛)—habitually visualizing how "the Buddha's body possesses the thirty-two marks and eighty minor characteristics, radiant with a hundred million points of light"—and he explicitly held that his practice "took meditative wisdom as its foundation" (以禪智為宗), that is, he incorporated buddha-recollection contemplation within the framework of meditative practice rather than treating it as a devotional rite separate from meditation. It was precisely because Huiyuan gave equal weight to meditation and wisdom that he was moved, during the Yixi reign, to invite Buddhabhadra—then in an awkward position owing to a controversy over "abandoning the established rules"—to take refuge at Mount Lu, translate scriptures, and teach meditation there, giving rise to the "Lushan Chan Jing." The meditation-and-wisdom tradition of Dao'an and Huiyuan thus forms a crucial historical hinge connecting the early meditation-sūtra systems of An Shigao and Kumārajīva to the subsequent Lushan Chan Jing and, further still, to the tradition of Pure Land buddha-recollection samādhi.

## Part Two: The Dominant Northern Dynasties Meditation Tradition, Developing in Parallel with the Bodhidharma Lineage—The Sengchou School and Its Contemporaries

If the Bodhidharma lineage eventually overtook all rivals to become the mainstream—indeed, virtually synonymous with—Chinese Buddhist discourse on practice, then in the period of its own formation, from the Northern and Southern Dynasties through the Sui-Tang transition, the actual situation was quite the reverse: the meditation lineage that truly held preeminent status at Ye (鄴城, capital of the Eastern Wei and Northern Qi), the great Buddhist center of the North, and that received sustained imperial patronage, was the tradition represented by Sengchou (僧稠)—not the Bodhidharma–Huike lineage. This historical fact was long marginalized within the Chan school's own internal narrative, and only regained its due historical weight through twentieth-century historical scholarship (notably Tang Yongtong's *History of Buddhism in the Wei, Jin, and Northern and Southern Dynasties* and Yinshun's *History of the Chan School*), archaeological discoveries of the past thirty years, and recent Western scholarship represented by Eric M. Greene.

### 1. Sengchou's Life and His "Four Foundations of Mindfulness" Meditation

Sengchou (僧稠, 480–560), lay surname Sun, was a native of Changli (in present-day Hebei). Born into a family of Imperial Academy scholars, he was well versed in the classics and histories in his youth. At age twenty-eight he left home under Master Sengshi at Jingming Monastery in Julu, and subsequently studied meditation under Daofang (道房), a disciple of "Chan Master Buddha" (Fotuo 佛陀, also called Batuo 跋陀, a Central Asian monk who came to China during the Northern Wei and served as founding abbot of Shaolin Monastery—a distinct lineage from the South Indian meditation tradition through which Bodhidharma himself trained). Sengchou eventually received Daofang's transmission and established his own tradition; the lineage may be summarized as: Chan Master Buddha—Daofang—Sengchou. Emperor Xiaoming of the Northern Wei summoned Sengchou from his retreat three times, each time without success; in the first year of the Yongxi reign of Emperor Xiaowu of the Northern Wei (532), a meditation hall was specially built for him in Shangshu Valley, where he gathered disciples under imperial patronage. Successive rulers of the Eastern Wei and Northern Qi treated him with great favor; Emperor Wenxuan of Northern Qi, Gao Yang, decreed in the third year of the Tianbao reign (552) that a retreat be built for him on Longshan, southwest of Ye, "extending a carriage-path up the mountain" and "establishing a treasury to fund his regular expenses." Sengchou thereby received the honorific title "National Preceptor, Great Virtuous One" (國師大德); his meditation teaching swept through the Ye region with a force unmatched by any other meditation master of the era—including Huike, disciple of Bodhidharma himself.

The core of Sengchou's meditation method was the "four foundations of mindfulness" (四念處: contemplating the body as impure, sensation as suffering, mind as impermanent, and phenomena as devoid of self), doctrinally grounded chiefly in the "four foundations of mindfulness" teachings found in the *Contemplation of Amitāyus Sūtra* (translated by Kālayaśas of the Liu Song) and the "Chapter on Sagely Practice" of the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra* (translated by Dharmakṣema of the Northern Liang)—a hybrid Hīnayāna-Mahāyāna system emphasizing concrete, step-by-step observation of body and mind, standing in sharp contrast to the Bodhidharma lineage's sudden-awakening orientation of "entering principle by means of the teaching" and "directly pointing to the mind." In the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*, in the general discussion of the "Practitioners of Meditation" chapter, the Tang-dynasty historian Daoxuan offers an assessment of the two that later scholars have often cited: he describes Bodhidharma's teaching as "wall-contemplation of the Mahāyāna, of the highest achievement, to which students of his generation looked up as though flocking to a marketplace," while at the same time placing Sengchou and Bodhidharma side by side as "two traditions": "Chou, cherishing the foundations of mindfulness, [his] pure model may be revered; Damo's dharma is of the void tradition, its dark purport deep and recondite" (稠、懷念處，清範可崇；摩法虛宗，玄旨幽賾)—meaning that Sengchou's four-foundations-of-mindfulness tradition was clear and readily followed ("its pure model may be revered"), while Bodhidharma's "tradition of the void" was subtle and difficult to grasp ("its dark purport deep and recondite"). Though this assessment comes from the very same Daoxuan whom later generations, including the Bodhidharma lineage's own heirs, would regard as an authority, it betrays not the slightest trace of the later tendency to treat the Bodhidharma lineage as the sole "orthodoxy"—it reflects, rather, the actual state of affairs in the minds of early Tang contemporaries, for whom the Sengchou and Bodhidharma traditions stood as equals, with Sengchou if anything the more highly esteemed of the two.

### 2. Archaeological Evidence: The Xiaonanhai Caves and Meditation Imagery

The historical standing of the Sengchou lineage has received important archaeological corroboration in recent decades. The Xiaonanhai Caves (also known as the Lingshan Temple Caves), southwest of Anyang in Henan, comprise three small grottoes—Central, Western, and Eastern—excavated between the first and sixth years of the Tianbao reign of the Northern Qi (550–555). According to the inscribed sutra text outside the caves, the Central Cave was initiated by the monk Fang of Lingshan Temple and others; "by the sixth year, the National Preceptor, Great Virtuous One, Chan Master Chou, further embellished and completed it, its features now complete." After Sengchou's death in 560 (the first year of the Qianming reign), his disciples, "following his contemplative method, accordingly carved the sūtra text into the stone in sections," inscribing the "Four Foundations of Mindfulness" chapter from the "Chapter on Sagely Practice" of the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra* together with verses from the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* onto the cliff face outside the caves—corresponding directly to the very meditation scriptures on which Sengchou relied during his lifetime. The interior walls of the three caves are carved respectively with images of Vairocana (or Śākyamuni), Amitābha (accompanied by relief carvings of the sixteen contemplations), and Maitreya (accompanied by relief carvings of his preaching); scholars have determined that the relief on the west wall of the Central Cave specifically depicts an Amitābha Pure Land transformation tableau based on the "sixteen contemplations" theme found in Kālayaśas's Liu-Song translation of the *Contemplation of Amitāyus Sūtra*—indicating that the Sengchou lineage's contemplative practice was not confined to the four foundations of mindfulness alone, but also encompassed the Pure Land visualization tradition, echoing the "visualization buddha-recollection" tradition of Huiyuan's lineage discussed above.

Scholars have pointed out that Buddhist meditation studies at Ye under the Northern Qi can broadly be divided into two systems: "the Buddhabhadra-lineage meditation method of the Yogācāra-master tradition" (i.e., the lineage descending from Chan Master Buddha through which Sengchou emerged, so named because its meditation method resembles the Sarvāstivādin Yogācāra-master system) and "the Bodhidharma-lineage meditation method of the Laṅkāvatāra school" (i.e., the tradition of Bodhidharma and Huike based on "entering principle by means of the teaching" via the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*)—with "the Buddhabhadra-lineage Sengchou meditation method standing alone at Ye, leading the fashion in Northern Dynasties meditation practice." The Sengchou lineage differed from the Bodhidharma lineage not only in doctrinal content but held an overwhelming advantage in geographic and political resources as well. This historical picture, supported by physical evidence—inscriptions, carved sūtras, and iconographic programs—stands in stark contrast to the impression of a lone-flourishing "Bodhidharma lineage" constructed by reliance on the later transmission-record literature. It is precisely this picture that Eric Greene's *Chan Before Chan: Meditation, Repentance, and Visionary Experience in Chinese Buddhism* (University of Hawai'i Press, 2021) and its companion volume *The Secrets of Buddhist Meditation: Visionary Meditation Texts from Early Medieval China* (same press, same year)—both recently praised as "paradigm-shifting" works of Western scholarship—labor to bring into focus. One of their central arguments is this: before the true formation of the Chan school in the eighth century, the way Chinese Buddhists understood and practiced "Chan" centered on obtaining "visionary" experiences that a senior meditation master could then interpret to determine the degree of purity or defilement in the practitioner's body and mind—an entire religious culture closely interwoven with rites of repentance (chanhui 懺悔)—rather than the linear developmental history, oriented toward "sudden awakening" as its sole goal, presupposed by the later Chan narrative.

### 3. The Continuation of the Sengchou Lineage and Other Northern Dynasties Meditation Masters

Sengchou's four principal disciples—Tanxun (曇詢, 520–599, whose own disciple was Zhenhui, 569–615), Sengyong (僧邕, 543–631, who later joined the Three Levels movement founded by Xinxing), Zhishun (智舜, 533–604, whose disciple was Zhizan), and Zhimin (whose disciple Zhishou, 567–635, was himself the teacher of Daoxuan—founder of the Nanshan Vinaya lineage in the Tang and author of the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*)—carried the lineage forward into the early Tang. The tangled web of connections between the Sengchou lineage, the Three Levels movement, and the Nanshan Vinaya lineage is itself an important thread for understanding early Tang Buddhist institutional history. It is worth noting that Daoxuan's own vinaya-lineage training descended from the Sengchou tradition rather than the Bodhidharma tradition—which may help explain why the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* is able to give a relatively even-handed account of the historical reality in which the Sengchou and Bodhidharma traditions coexisted, each commanding its own respect.

Another important Northern Dynasties meditation master, roughly contemporary with but slightly later than Sengchou, was Sengshi (僧實, 476–563), who was held in great esteem by the Northern Zhou court. Tradition holds that during the Baoding era (561–565), Emperor Taizu of Zhou remarked, "This master's talent runs deep and his virtue is great; he ought to shelter both clergy and laity, that we may exalt the rites due him," and that upon his death an imperial decree ordered that "his likeness be painted and enshrined at the Great Field of Merit Monastery." His historical standing was comparable to, if slightly beneath, Sengchou's—another meditation authority who, independent of the Bodhidharma lineage, received deep veneration from the Northern Dynasties court.

It is further worth noting that recent scholarship (including the relevant chapters of Greene's *Chan Before Chan*) has offered a new interpretation of a passage in Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* often misread as "intended to elevate the Bodhidharma lineage": some research suggests that the actual target of Daoxuan's criticism in this passage may not have been Bodhidharma and his followers at all, but rather the Three Levels movement (三階教) founded by Xinxing (信行, 540–594), which likewise had lineage entanglements with the Sengchou tradition. This suggests that the debates within seventh-century Buddhism over the meaning and legitimacy of "Chan" involved far more parties than a simple binary of "Bodhidharma lineage versus non-Bodhidharma lineage," a matter that still awaits further clarification by scholars.

### 4. The Dilun and Shelun Masters: Intersections with Northern Dynasties Meditation Culture

Roughly contemporaneous with the Sengchou lineage, the Northern Dynasties also saw the emergence of two Yogācāra scholastic lineages: the "Dilun masters" (地論師, divided into Southern and Northern Xiangzhou branches), centered on the study of Vasubandhu's *Daśabhūmika-śāstra*, and the "Shelun masters" (攝論師), centered on the study of Asaṅga's *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* as transmitted through Paramārtha. Though these were scholastic lineages known chiefly for doctrinal exegesis rather than for meditation methods per se, their theories of the ālayavijñāna and tathāgatagarbha-based dependent origination in fact provided important doctrinal support for Northern Dynasties meditative practice, and there was considerable personal interaction and mutual influence between the two currents—for instance, the Dilun masters Daoping and Lingyu, closely associated with the construction of the Xiaonanhai and Lingquan Temple caves, carried out their own cave-construction and sūtra-inscription activities immediately adjacent to the Sengchou-lineage meditation caves, reflecting a Northern Dynasties monastic ecology in which the "doctrinally-oriented Dilun and Shelun masters" and the "practice-oriented meditation masters" were by no means sharply separated, but rather formed an interpenetrating community of thought occupying the same physical space.

### 5. The Dunhuang Manuscripts and Modern Scholarly History: From Hu Shi to Eric Greene

The rediscovery of the Sengchou lineage—and, more broadly, of the entire "pre-Bodhidharma / non-Bodhidharma" meditation tradition—owes its foundation to the early-twentieth-century excavation of the Dunhuang sūtra cave. Among the large cache of manuscripts removed by Paul Pelliot, Aurel Stein, and others were a considerable number of early meditation texts and "pre-history of Chan" documents previously known only from catalogue entries, their original texts long since lost—providing first-hand material for rewriting this chapter of history.

In the scholarly lineage that has drawn on this material to reconstruct the pre-history of Chan and the process by which the Bodhidharma lineage took shape, Hu Shi (胡適, 1891–1962) was the pioneer, applying modern historical-critical methods systematically to the Dunhuang Chan manuscripts for the first time. His conclusions—such as his account of the pivotal role Shenhui played in establishing the Southern School's orthodoxy, and his textual-critical work on the evolution of the various recensions of the *Platform Sūtra*—drew notable criticism, on methodological grounds (an overemphasis on historical-critical method, in the view of critics like D.T. Suzuki, who held that the essence of Chan awakening could not be reduced to historical fact), yet his pioneering contribution remains undeniable. Later, Master Yinshun (印順, 1906–2005), drawing on decades of scholarly labor, completed his *History of the Chan School: From Indian Dhyāna to Chinese Chan* (1971; he was awarded a doctorate in literature from Taishō University in Japan in 1973 on the strength of this work), which offered a systematic account, still authoritative today, of the Bodhidharma tradition, the East Mountain Teaching, the rise of the Oxhead School, the formation of the *Platform Sūtra*, and the differentiation of Caoxi Chan. In parallel, the Japanese scholars Yanagida Seizan (柳田聖山, *A Study of Early Chan Historical Texts*, 1967), Ui Hakuju (宇井伯壽, *Studies in Chan History*), and Sekiguchi Shindai (關口真大, *A Study of the Great Master Bodhidharma*), among others, produced a series of philological findings that corroborated and advanced Yinshun's research in tandem, jointly establishing the basic philological framework for the study of the pre-history of Chan and early meditation methods that continues to this day.

Into the twenty-first century, a new generation of scholars represented by Eric Greene (a scholar at Yale University specializing in medieval Chinese Buddhist history) has, building on and fully digesting these philological findings, further introduced perspectives from religious history, art history (such as the iconographic studies of the Xiaonanhai caves discussed above), and even semiotic theory, systematically reassessing the central place occupied by the Sengchou lineage, "visionary" meditation practice, and repentance ritual within medieval Chinese Buddhist practice culture. This body of scholarship has been praised for having "dissolved the long-standing teleological narrative that treated early medieval meditation practice merely as a 'preparatory stage' for the mature Chan school." It may fairly be said that a complete "history of meditation traditions beyond Huineng's Southern School" is, to a great extent, the product of this century of cumulative work in Dunhuang studies, cave-archaeology, and philology.

## Part Three: Bodhidharma Through Hongren—The Shared Early Teachings and the Formation of the "East Mountain Teaching"

Before turning to the Northern School, the Oxhead School, the Sichuan lineages, and other early Chan traditions "eliminated" from the later Southern School orthodox narrative, it is necessary first to return to the "shared patriarchs" themselves—Bodhidharma, Huike, Sengcan, Daoxin, and Hongren—figures acknowledged by both the Southern and Northern Schools alike. For it is precisely their individual meditation practices and teachings that, once the later genealogical narrative of "a special transmission outside the teachings, mind-to-mind transmission" had crystallized, came to be simplified, or even retroactively recast as mere stepping-stones toward Huineng's doctrine of "sudden awakening and seeing one's nature"—their own independent intellectual profiles and methods of practice long failing to receive the independent attention they deserve. Twentieth-century philological scholarship, particularly the discovery of the Dunhuang manuscripts and the systematic work of scholars such as Hu Shi and Yinshun, allows us to recover, to a considerable degree, the original character of each patriarch's own teaching.

### 1. Bodhidharma: The Two Entrances and Four Practices, and Wall-Contemplation

The earliest surviving text that can be confirmed as deriving from Bodhidharma himself (or at least directly recorded by disciples close to him) is the *Erru Sixing Lun* (二入四行論, "Treatise on the Two Entrances and Four Practices"), prefaced and compiled by Bodhidharma's disciple Tanlin (曇林). It was first brought to light in 1935 by the Japanese scholar D.T. Suzuki on the basis of Dunhuang manuscripts (including Pelliot Chinois 99), and subsequently critically edited by Yanagida Seizan; it stands as the most authoritative primary source for the study of Bodhidharma's meditation teaching, far more reliable than the later transmission-record legends.

According to this treatise, Bodhidharma's teaching can be summarized as "two entrances" and "four practices." The **entrance of principle** (理入) refers to "entering principle by means of the teaching" (藉教悟宗)—deeply believing that all sentient beings share the same true nature, obscured only by adventitious defilement and false thought, such that it cannot manifest; if one can abandon the false and return to the true, "abiding fixedly in wall-contemplation, without self and other, ordinary and sage being equal and one, firmly abiding without shifting, no longer following after written teachings," one then tallies silently with the principle—this is the "entrance of principle," and it is likewise the basis for what later came to be called the method of "wall-contemplation" (壁觀). The **four practices** are: the practice of requiting enmity (accepting the suffering of past karmic retribution willingly, without resentment), the practice of according with conditions (neither gain nor loss disturbing the mind), the practice of seeking nothing (cutting off craving), and the practice of according with the Dharma (acting in accordance with the principle of one's own inherently pure nature). According to Tanlin's preface, Bodhidharma taught his two disciples Huike and Daoyu directly with a four-phrase formula: "thus stilling the mind, thus arousing practice, thus according with things, thus employing skillful means"—"stilling the mind" refers to wall-contemplation, "arousing practice" to the four practices, "according with things" to guarding against slander and ill will, and "skillful means" to relinquishing attachment. Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* records that Bodhidharma "used this teaching to enlighten the land of Wei; those with genuine understanding who received his teaching attained realization, and his words were recorded and circulated in the world"—showing that his teaching was indeed committed to writing and circulated at the time, rather than being wholly "without reliance on words," a point often overlooked by the later Southern School narrative's overemphasis on "a special transmission outside the teachings." Although Bodhidharma's meditation teaching is renowned for its sudden-awakening orientation of "directly pointing to the mind," "wall-contemplation" as a concrete method of seated meditation carries a considerable degree of śamatha-vipassanā coloring, and remains subtly distinct from a purely sudden-awakening approach unmediated by any expedient means—a point that repays close attention in later accounts of the evolution of Chan thought.

### 2. Huike and Sengcan: Gaps in the Historical Record and the Layered Accretion of Legend

Huike's (慧可, 487–593) activities are recorded in Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*. Well versed in Confucian learning in his youth, he traveled at age forty to Mount Song to study under Bodhidharma for six years, fully receiving his teaching. He subsequently faced repeated censure, as his contemporaries could not accept Bodhidharma's meditation teaching; during Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou's suppression of Buddhism (574), he and Tanlin, another of Bodhidharma's disciples, worked together to "protect the scriptures and images," and in 577 he retreated into seclusion at Mount Wangong in Shuzhou, where he ordained Sengcan. It is worth noting that the widely circulated later legend of "Huike severing his arm to seek the Dharma" is, according to modern scholarly analysis, very likely a conflation of Tanlin's own genuine misfortune—he was attacked by bandits and had an arm severed, earning him the epithet "Armless Lin"—with the narrative of Huike's quest for the Dharma. The original text of Daoxuan's *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* biography of Huike records only that, in his later years, Huike "suffered the severing of an arm by bandits, and through the Dharma governed his mind, feeling no pain"; it makes no mention of any episode of "standing in the snow and severing his arm" in the course of seeking the Dharma. This episode was in fact added through successive retellings in later texts—the *Baolin Zhuan*, the *Lengqie Shizi Ji*, the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu*, and so on—a textbook example of the "layered accretion" phenomenon in Chan historical sources.

The historical record for Sengcan is even thinner: the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan* offers no independent biography of him at all; he is mentioned only in passing, within the appended "Remaining Accounts" section of Daoxin's biography, where it is noted that "there were also two monks, of unknown origin, who entered Mount Wangong in Shuzhou to cultivate meditation practice in seclusion; [Daoxin], hearing of this, went to join them, and thereupon received the Dharma from them, following and studying under them for ten years." Yinshun, on the basis of circumstantial evidence that Sengcan was known to have frequented nearby Mount Du, close to Mount Wangong, inferred that these "two monks" likely included Sengcan, concluding that "it is credible that Daoxin received the Dharma from Sengcan"—but this remains, in the end, an inference from geographical circumstance rather than a direct textual confirmation. It may fairly be said that, of the five-generation transmission from Bodhidharma to Hongren, apart from the somewhat thin documentation of the link between Sengcan and Daoxin, the master-disciple relationships of the other generations are on the whole documentable in the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*; yet the biographical details of each patriarch remain "largely pieced together, vague in particulars, and riddled with internal contradiction"—as the scholar Du Jiwen once lamented regarding the historical records left by the Chan tradition itself: to believe the Chan school's own records entirely, taking them as unerring historical fact, is certainly to be led astray; yet to set them aside entirely makes research on Chan impossible in the first place. This dilemma is precisely the methodological premise that any study of early Chan history must remain constantly conscious of. The earliest surviving stone inscription recording a Chan lineage, the *Epitaph for the Chan Master Faru of Mount Song* (唐中嶽沙門釋法如禪師行狀碑), erected in 689 CE (the first year of the Yongchang reign of the Tang), states explicitly: "[Bodhidharma] entered Wei and transmitted to Ke; Ke transmitted to Can; Can transmitted to Xin; Xin transmitted to Ren; Ren transmitted to Ru"—that is, Bodhidharma, Huike, Sengcan, Daoxin, Hongren, culminating in Faru (法如) himself. This stele was originally erected to establish Faru's status as the legitimate "sixth-generation" heir to Hongren's robe and bowl. Intriguingly, Faru died in precisely the same year the stele was erected, 689; thereafter the Chan community in the central plains, having lost its point of reference, turned instead to the increasingly prominent Shenxiu—Faru may thus fairly be called a nearly-forgotten "candidate Sixth Patriarch" in the history of the Chan school, and it is surely no coincidence that his "elimination" from the record coincided almost exactly with the rise of Shenxiu's Northern School (as will be discussed further below). This same stele also cites the aforementioned preface to the *Lushan Chan Jing* to trace an even earlier Indian lineage of transmission (Buddha—Ānanda—Madhyāntika—Śāṇavāsa...)—showing that the Faru lineage likewise invoked the authority of this very "non-Bodhidharma" scripture to bolster its own standing, a textual connection worth noting in its own right. In any case, this shared segment of the lineage from Bodhidharma to Hongren was subsequently acknowledged uniformly by the Northern School (which traced its transmission from Hongren through Shenxiu), the Southern School (through Huineng), and the Oxhead School (through Daoxin to Farong) alike—though scholars point out that this shared genealogy itself "must have been retrospectively constructed after Hongren's time." Even so, this fact suggests that the transmission prior to Hongren already carried a considerable degree of public credibility at the time.

### 3. Daoxin and Hongren: The True Founding of the "East Mountain Teaching"

If Bodhidharma, Huike, and Sengcan represent, for the most part, a period of itinerant, individual "ascetic-practice" style cultivation, with relatively loosely organized followings, then a fundamental transformation began with Daoxin (道信, 580–651): he led his disciples to settle permanently at Twin Peaks Mountain (Shuangfeng Shan) in Huangmei, Hubei—a break from the previous Bodhidharma-lineage pattern of having "no fixed abode"—clearing land, achieving self-sufficiency, and gathering a settled community that, over several decades, grew to between five hundred and a thousand members. His disciple Hongren (弘忍, 601–674) subsequently established a separate practice center at Fengmao Mountain, east of Twin Peaks, known as "East Mountain Monastery" (東山寺)—hence the lineage of Daoxin and Hongren together came to be called the "East Mountain Teaching" (東山法門). Scholarly consensus over recent decades has increasingly converged on an important point: rather than saying Chinese Chan "was founded by Bodhidharma" or "was founded by Huineng," it would be more accurate to say that it was truly "founded by Daoxin and brought to completion by Hongren"—it was the sustained efforts of Daoxin and Hongren, over more than half a century, in both doctrinal innovation and monastic organizational reform, that marked the genuine historical establishment of Chan as a mature, sinicized new school of Buddhism.

Daoxin authored the *Rudao Anxin Yao Fangbian Famen* (入道安心要方便法門, "Essential Expedient Methods for Entering the Path and Stilling the Mind"; the full text is preserved through its citation in Jingjue's *Lengqie Shizi Ji*). His meditation teaching, as summarized in the *Lengqie Shizi Ji*, rests on two textual pillars: "Following the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*, the mind of all buddhas is foremost" (inheriting the Bodhidharma lineage's tradition of taking the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* as its scripture of confirmation for "entering principle by means of the teaching"), and "Following the *Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra Spoken by Mañjuśrī*, the one-practice samādhi—that is, the mind that recollects the Buddha is itself Buddha, while a mind of false thought is that of an ordinary being" (absorbing the "one-practice samādhi" (一行三昧) method from the Prajñāpāramitā corpus and fusing it with buddha-recollection practice). It is clear that Daoxin's meditation teaching had already explicitly integrated the Prajñāpāramitā-derived "one-practice samādhi" with contemplative buddha-recollection into a single system; his concrete expedient methods of "guarding the one" and "observing the mind" demonstrate a considerable degree of methodological overlap and mutual borrowing with the Pure Land buddha-recollection samādhi tradition discussed above, and with the Tiantai contemplative system discussed below—both "non-Southern-School" traditions in their own right—further confirming this study's introductory point: the historical boundaries between the various meditation traditions are far more fluid and interpenetrating than later sectarian ideology has depicted them to be.

Hongren inherited and further developed Daoxin's meditation teaching; his thought is concentrated in the *Xiuxin Yao Lun* (修心要論, "Treatise on the Essentials of Cultivating the Mind"), recorded by his disciples. Its core teaching is "guarding the fundamental true mind" (守本真心, abbreviated "guarding the mind," 守心): it holds that the mind of sentient beings is originally pure, obscured only by the covering of false thought; one need only "guard the fundamental true mind, so that false thought does not arise," to attain "clear and complete realization and knowledge, ultimate liberation." Compared to Bodhidharma's "wall-contemplation" and Daoxin's "purifying the mind through buddha-recollection," this teaching further condenses and simplifies practical cultivation into a still more direct essential formula of "guarding the mind"—leaving ample interpretive room for the further simplifications and developments that Shenxiu's lineage, and Huineng's lineage, would each independently pursue afterward. According to the *Chuanfa Baoji* (傳法寶紀), "by the time of Ren [Hongren], Ru [Faru], and Datong [Shenxiu], the Dharma-gate had opened wide, admitting all capacities without discrimination, all alike swiftly reciting the Buddha's name, that the mind be purified"—showing that, at the outset of the East Mountain lineage's transmission under Hongren, buddha-recollection practice remained closely integrated with meditative instruction, not yet sharply distinguished from it, an interesting contrast to the later historical trajectory in which vocal-recitation buddha-name practice gradually became independent of the Chan school. Hongren's disciples were numerous and talented; according to the *Lengqie Shizi Ji*, the *Lidai Fabao Ji*, and other texts, his "ten great disciples" are typically listed as including Shenxiu, Zhishen, Lao'an (Huian), Faru, Xuanze, and Huineng, among others. Hongren himself is recorded as judging that Shenxiu had "grasped the essential purport" of the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* most fully in accord with his own understanding, that Zhishen and the Registrar Liu were "possessed of literary talent," that Huian was "of lofty conduct," and that Faru, Huineng, and Zhide were each "capable of serving as a teacher in his own region"—an evaluation that in itself reveals the historical reality that Hongren's disciples each excelled in their own particular strengths, with no single disciple presupposed as the inevitable "sole orthodox" successor. This laid the internal groundwork for the East Mountain Teaching's rapid subsequent differentiation into the Northern School, the Southern School, the Oxhead School, the Sichuan lineages, and other parallel branches.

## Part Four: The Northern School—Shenxiu's Lineage and the Rise and Fall of Its Historical Standing

Among the various branches into which the "East Mountain Teaching" differentiated after Hongren's death, the one that initially carried the greatest weight, and that for a long period enjoyed the favor of the highest levels of the Tang court, was not the "Southern School" later exalted above all others, but rather the "Northern School" represented by Shenxiu (神秀)—this is the most crucial, and also the most easily obscured by conventional popular accounts of Chan history, chapter in the story of "traditions beyond Huineng."

### 1. Shenxiu's Life: "Dharma-Master of the Two Capitals, Teacher to Three Emperors"

Shenxiu (神秀, 606–706), lay surname Li, was extensively read in the classics and histories in his youth. Upon reaching adulthood he left home, and at age fifty traveled to Mount Dongshan in Huangmei to pay his respects to Hongren, undertaking such labor as gathering firewood and drawing water in his quest for the Dharma; he won Hongren's deep esteem and occupied the position of "chief seat" (上座) among Hongren's disciples—Hongren judged that Shenxiu had "grasped the essential purport" of the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* most fully in accord with his own understanding. After Hongren's death, Shenxiu taught for several decades at Yuquan Monastery on Mount Dangyang in Jiangling, his reputation gradually growing; around the Jiushi era of the Wu Zhou, Empress Wu Zetian dispatched an envoy to welcome him to the capital, where he was "carried on a palanquin up to the throne hall, [the empress] herself bowing to him in reverence." Thereafter, across the reigns of Empress Wu, Emperor Zhongzong, and Emperor Ruizong, he was honored as preceptor to the imperial house, earning the title "Dharma-Master of the Two Capitals, Teacher to Three Emperors" (兩京法主，三帝國師)—that is, the acknowledged teaching-master for the monastic and lay communities of both capitals, Chang'an and Luoyang, and the personal teacher of three successive emperors: Empress Wu, Zhongzong, and Ruizong. Upon his death he was granted the posthumous title "Chan Master of Great Penetration" (大通禪師). In his lifetime Shenxiu's prestige was such that, for a time, "all within the two capitals revered Shenxiu"; the situation in which he and his fellow-disciple Huineng, teaching in the far south of Lingnan, held sway over their respective northern and southern spheres came to be known as "Neng in the South, Xiu in the North." Yet in terms of actual social influence and institutional resources at the time, Shenxiu's lineage held an overwhelming advantage—a structural parallel to the earlier advantage the Sengchou lineage held over the Bodhidharma–Huike lineage in the Northern Dynasties, discussed above: at multiple junctures in the history of Chan, "orthodoxy" and "mainstream status"—the lineage retrospectively sanctioned within the tradition's own narrative, and the lineage that actually held sway in its own time—have frequently failed to coincide.

Shenxiu had nineteen recorded disciples, the most eminent among them Puji of Mount Song and Yifu of the capital; Puji in particular came to be regarded by his contemporaries as "the orthodox heir of the Bodhidharma lineage." Puji's disciple Dōsen (道璿) later transmitted Northern School Chan teaching to Japan—yet another, independent channel (alongside the later-discussed transmission routes of Tiantai and Esoteric Buddhism) by which Chan itself spread eastward to Japan.

### 2. Shenxiu's Meditation Teaching: The *Guanxin Lun* and the *Dasheng Wusheng Fangbian Men*

Scholarship today generally accepts two works as genuinely authored by Shenxiu himself: the *Guanxin Lun* (觀心論, "Treatise on Contemplating the Mind") and the *Dasheng Wusheng Fangbian Men* (大乘無生方便門, "Mahāyāna Gate of Expedient Means for Non-Arising")—along with the shorter *Exhortation to Goodness by Reverend Xiu*; while other works traditionally attributed to Shenxiu, such as the *Yuanming Lun* and the *Dahua Yan Jing Shu*, have been determined by scholarship to be the work of other hands, not genuinely Shenxiu's own.

The *Guanxin Lun* centers on the thesis that "the ten thousand dharmas are mind alone" (萬法唯心), holding that sustained contemplation of the falsity and unreality of one's own mind enables the severing of afflictions and entry into suchness; it repeatedly emphasizes essential practices such as "leaving thought" (離念), "no-mind" (無心), and "quiescent awareness" (寂知), indicating a path of practice that proceeds through introspective contemplation, gradually approaching the source of the nature of mind. The *Dasheng Wusheng Fangbian Men* is traditionally attributed to the lecture records of Shenxiu's lineage under Puji, Jingxian, and others; its content consists of a series of expedient methods for guiding students into "non-arising" (無生, i.e., the reality of nirvāṇa), encompassing stages of leaving thought, non-intentionality, "knowing," "understanding," and "seeing the nature." The text states explicitly: "The buddhas and tathāgatas possess a great expedient means for entering the path—a single thought of pure mind suddenly transcends the stage of Buddhahood"—it is worth noting that the phrase "suddenly transcends the stage of Buddhahood" (頓超佛地) already carries a considerable coloring of "suddenness," demonstrating that Northern School meditation teaching was by no means entirely the simplistically "gradual cultivation, gradual awakening" tradition that later Southern School critics portrayed it as, but rather possessed its own internal logic of "manifesting the sudden by means of the gradual." Drawing on both treatises together with the sayings recorded in the *Lengqie Shizi Ji*, scholars generally summarize Shenxiu's lineage's stages of practice and realization as a progressively deepening path—"guarding the mind → contemplating the mind → observing the mind → observing that there is nowhere to abide"—ultimately pointing toward "the mutual extinction of subject and object" (this teaching, articulated by Shenxiu's second-generation disciples Jiangmozang and Puji as a further development of their master's teaching, describes it as "fixing the mind and entering samādhi, abiding in mind and observing purity, arousing the mind to illuminate outwardly, gathering the mind to verify inwardly"). Compared with the Southern School's simplified approach of "directly pointing to the mind, seeing the nature and becoming Buddha" under Huineng, this indeed presents a more elaborate and meticulously constructed progressive structure—yet this very "elaborateness" reflects its own soteriological rationale, that of "guarding against the pitfalls of wild Chan by means of graduated practice," and should not simply be dismissed, from the Southern School's own standpoint, as "gradual" and therefore inferior to "sudden."

### 3. The Historical Construction of the "Southern Sudden, Northern Gradual" Narrative: Shenhui's Assembly at Huatai and the Building of Southern Orthodoxy

The reason Northern School meditation teaching came to occupy a long-marginalized—indeed, nearly "erased"—position in the later narration of Chinese Buddhist history has its historical turning point concentrated around the twenty-second year of the Kaiyuan era (730 CE): Huineng's disciple Heze Shenhui (荷澤神會) publicly convened an "unrestricted assembly" (無遮大會) at Dayun Monastery in Huatai, Henan, fiercely attacking the Northern School lineage under Puji for having "a lineage that was but a side-branch, and a Dharma-gate that was merely gradual," and vigorously championing Huineng as the true "orthodox" Sixth Patriarch who had genuinely received Hongren's robe and bowl, insisting that only Huineng's "sudden awakening" lineage represented the authentic transmission of Bodhidharma's Chan. Although this debate may not have immediately overturned the Northern School's actual social standing during Shenhui's own lifetime (scholars note that, while the account by the Japanese pilgrim-monk Eisai in his *Kōzen Gokokuron*—"the followers of sudden and gradual regarded each other as enemies, the Southern and Northern Schools as hostile as Chu and Han"—paints a dramatic picture, a comparison with the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu*, the *Chan Yuan Zhuquan Ji Duxu*, and other texts suggests the actual degree of contention between the two schools at the time may not have been quite as fierce as later imagination would have it), the framework of "Southern orthodoxy" that Shenhui constructed nonetheless achieved, through the formation and transmission of the *Platform Sūtra* and its cumulative adoption in the later transmission-record literature, an overwhelming dominance over the historical record from the mid-to-late Tang onward. The Northern School's own textual corpus gradually disappeared from circulation in China proper as well, only to be recovered—and its original character made visible to us again—through the reappearance of the Dunhuang manuscripts in the twentieth century.

This process of "orthodox-narrative construction" has itself drawn the sustained attention of successive generations of modern scholars: Hu Shi, drawing on Dunhuang manuscripts of Shenhui's recorded sayings and related documents held in Paris and London, was the first to systematically demonstrate the pivotal—one might even say decisive—historical role Shenhui played in establishing the "orthodoxy" of the Southern School. Although his conclusions provoked, on methodological grounds, a well-known controversy with D.T. Suzuki and other scholars working from within the tradition, they nonetheless inaugurated the modern scholarly paradigm of reassessing early Chan history through philological criticism; the historical picture of early Chan history—"a North-South split, with the Southern School emerging victorious though the later-arising party"—was subsequently brought into sharper focus through the further refined scholarship of Yinshun, Yanagida Seizan, and others. In 1986, the American scholar John McRae published *The Northern School and the Formation of Early Ch'an Buddhism* (University of Hawai'i Press), the first Western monograph-scale systematic reassessment of the doctrinal content and historical standing of Northern School meditation teaching, arguing against a simplistic reduction of Shenxiu's lineage's actual practice-system to the label of "gradual cultivation"—a foundational work in contemporary Northern School studies. It is also worth noting that recent scholarship examining the relationship between the late-Ming *Śūraṅgama Sūtra* and Northern School Chan has suggested that Northern School meditation teaching, as an independent intellectual resource, may not have "utterly vanished" after the Tang dynasty in quite the way traditional Chan historiography implies—an issue that still awaits further textual excavation and scholarly clarification, and which is noted here simply to illustrate the open-endedness of historical conclusions.

## Part Five: The Oxhead School—Farong's Prajñā-Based Chan

Flourishing at almost the same time as the Northern School, yet developing along a Prajñāpāramitā-Madhyamaka line of thought entirely different from the tathāgatagarbha-based mind-nature theory of the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*, was the "Oxhead School" (Niutou Zong 牛頭宗, also called Oxhead Chan), active in the region of Jinling (present-day Nanjing), with Farong (法融) as its founding patriarch—so named because Farong resided at Mount Oxhead (Niutou Shan, in present-day Jiangning District, Nanjing). It emerged as a side-branch in the sixth-to-seventh centuries and flourished greatly from the early through mid-Tang; it is sometimes paired with, and set alongside, Hongren's "East Mountain School" in later accounts.

### 1. Farong's Life and the Epithet "The Bodhidharma of Eastern China"

Farong (法融, 594–657), lay surname Wei, was a native of Yanling in Runzhou (present-day Jiangsu). At nineteen he left home at Fengle Monastery on Mount Mao, studying under Master Daming (also recorded as Master Jiong), a disciple in the Sanlun (Three Treatises, Madhyamaka) lineage under Xinghuang Falang, systematically studying the foundational Sanlun texts—the *Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra*, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, the *Śata-śāstra*, the *Dvādaśanikāya-śāstra*, and the *Śata-śāstra Vaipulya*. He later moved to Youqi Monastery on Mount Oxhead, spending eight years in intensive study of the "seven treasuries" of scriptures housed at Fokū Monastery; his learning was profound, his reputation grew, and students gathered around him in great numbers, forming a large and thriving assembly. Later generations honored him with the epithet "the Bodhidharma of Eastern China" (東夏之達摩)—testament to the exalted standing he held within the Chan community of his day.

Whether Farong actually received direct transmission from Daoxin, the Fourth Patriarch of the East Mountain Teaching, is a major point of contention in Oxhead School studies. Traditional sources within the tradition (such as Guifeng Zongmi's *Chanmen Shizi Chengxi Tu*, "Chart of the Master-Disciple Succession of the Chan Schools") record that Farong, having spent years deeply immersed in Prajñāpāramitā-based emptiness teaching and having "already awakened to the fundamental emptiness of all dharmas," received confirmation of his understanding from Daoxin, who crossed the river in person to meet him and said, "This Dharma, from ancient times, has been entrusted to only one person at a time; I have already bequeathed it to Hongren, so you may establish your own separate branch"—thereupon allowing Farong to found an independent lineage at Mount Oxhead, hence the traditional view of the Oxhead School as "a side-branch issuing from the Fourth Patriarch Daoxin's lineage." However, according to the textual-critical findings of Master Yinshun and other modern scholars: Farong was a contemporary of Daoxuan, author of the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*, yet Daoxuan's work makes no mention whatsoever of Daoxin having confirmed Farong's realization; moreover, the actual intellectual origins and doctrinal system of Farong, founding patriarch of the Oxhead School, align far more closely with the Sanlun tradition, and are in fact rather distant from the mind-nature theory of the Bodhidharma–Laṅkāvatāra lineage. On this basis, the story of "Daoxin's transmission of the Dharma to Farong" is very likely a legend fabricated and embellished by later generations in order to fold the Oxhead School into the orthodox Bodhidharma genealogy, rather than genuine history; the intellectual origins of the Oxhead School are more accurately located as "a regional school that arose as a side-branch of the Sanlun tradition, fused with Southern-style meditative practice, and developed independently," rather than as a direct branch of the Bodhidharma lineage. This textual-critical conclusion is precisely the reason this study treats the Oxhead School as an independent, major chapter in its own right, rather than confining it to a boundary-case footnote.

### 2. "Originally Nothing the Matter": The Prajñā Coloring of Oxhead Meditation Teaching

The doctrinal position of the Oxhead School directly inherits the Sanlun lineage of the *Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra* and the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*; its core thesis is that "all is empty" and "originally nothing the matter" (本來無事). Farong's *Xin Ming* (心銘, "Inscription on the Mind") declares: "The nature of mind is unborn—what need is there of knowing and seeing? Originally there is not a single dharma—what is there to speak of cultivating and refining?" and "Bodhi is originally possessed—there is no need to guard it; affliction is originally non-existent—there is no need to eliminate it." This mode of expression—"not cultivating yet cultivating," "requiring no contrived antidote"—bears an evident precursory relationship to the doctrine of sudden awakening later championed by the Southern School, and merits particular attention: in other words, although Oxhead meditation teaching developed independently of the Bodhidharma lineage, its spiritual temperament of "sweeping away all attachment through Prajñā" objectively laid a considerable degree of intellectual groundwork for the later formation of the Southern School's sudden-awakening ethos. The relationship between the two is one of "parallel, independent development coupled with mutual interpenetration and influence," rather than simple "genealogical transmission" or "no relation whatsoever."

Beyond the *Xin Ming*, another important surviving text recording Farong's meditation teaching is the *Jueguan Lun* (絕觀論, "Treatise on the Transcendence of Contemplation," presented in a master-disciple question-and-answer format), which reemerged in the early twentieth century through the collation of several manuscript copies held in Paris and Beijing, dated to approximately 794 CE. Guifeng Zongmi, Fifth Patriarch of the Huayan school, classified the Oxhead School in his doxographical *Chanmen Shizi Chengxi Tu* as the teaching of "extinguishing all and relying on nothing" (泯絕無寄宗)—meaning a school that holds that "all dharmas are to be regarded as a dream; originally there is nothing the matter, and mind and its objects are fundamentally quiescent... having realized that originally there is nothing the matter, principle is lost and sentiment is forgotten; when sentiment is forgotten, the cause of suffering is severed"—a teaching that takes "forgetting sentiment" as its fundamental method of practice. Another noteworthy theoretical innovation of the Oxhead School lies in its doctrine that "even the insentient possess Buddha-nature" (無情有性)—holding that even insentient things such as plants and stones possess Buddha-nature—a thesis later elaborated further by Zhanran, Ninth Patriarch of the Tiantai school, and one that exerted a profound influence on Chinese Buddhist mind-nature theory from the Song dynasty onward (though the precise intellectual line of transmission between the two remains a matter of scholarly discussion).

### 3. Lineage Transmission and Its Ultimate Historical Fate

The Oxhead lineage is traditionally said to have descended from Farong through Zhiyan, Huifang, Fachi, Zhiwei, Huizhong and Xuansu, Daoqin, and Daolin—eight generations in all, spanning roughly two centuries, with Niaoke Daolin (鳥窠道林, 741–824—the celebrated "Bird's Nest Chan Master," famous for his exchange with the poet Bai Juyi on "avoiding all evil, upholding all good") generally regarded as the last figure of significant influence in this line. The Oxhead School flourished during the mid-Tang, but because its doctrine and soteriological orientation bore considerable resemblance and affinity to those of the Southern School, which subsequently achieved overwhelming dominance, it was ultimately "absorbed into the Chan school, its independent transmission coming to an end," its identity as an independent school gradually dissolving into the mainstream Chan narrative. This trajectory—an identity rendered obscure precisely through being absorbed and digested for being *too similar* to the eventual victor—forms an intriguing contrast with the fate of Northern School Chan discussed above, which was instead fiercely criticized and marginalized precisely *because* its position diverged from the victor's. Together, the two cases reveal that the rise and fall of a Chan branch's standing in the historical record depends not entirely on the relative depth or sophistication of its own doctrine, but is profoundly shaped by any number of contingent historical factors—region, political resources, the vigor of one's later disciples, and indeed the degree of resemblance or divergence from the eventual "victor's" own narrative.

## Part Six: The Sichuan Lineages—The Jingzhong and Baotang Schools, and Their Transmission into Tibet

Among the branches into which the East Mountain Teaching differentiated, one possesses a particularly distinct regional character, and has drawn increasing scholarly attention in recent years owing to the excavation of Dunhuang Tibetan-language documents: the "Sichuan lineage" (蜀地禪系), founded by Zhishen (智詵), one of Hongren's ten great disciples, and subsequently developing within Sichuan into the two branches of the Jingzhong (淨眾) and Baotang (保唐) schools. Not only did this lineage develop its own distinctive character within China proper, forming a three-way rivalry with Huineng's Southern School and Shenxiu's Northern School, it also spread westward along the Hexi Corridor into Tibet, becoming directly caught up in the famous "Debate of Sudden and Gradual" (頓漸之諍, also called the Lhasa Debate or the Samye Debate) in late-eighth-century Tibetan Buddhist history—one of the most dramatic episodes of cross-cultural transmission in the entire "Chan traditions beyond Huineng" story.

### 1. Zhishen and Chuji: The Robe of Faith Travels East to West Sichuan

Zhishen was one of Hongren's ten great disciples; after Hongren's death he returned to Sichuan, where he founded the Sichuan lineage. According to the Sichuan lineage's own account of its transmission, before his death Zhishen bequeathed to his dharma-heir Chuji (處寂) the robe of faith (信衣) reputedly bestowed by Empress Wu Zetian, symbolizing legitimate succession from Bodhidharma. However, because the Zhishen lineage possessed only this robe as a physical token of transmission, without having received the "mind-to-mind seal" of confirmation from the preceding generation of patriarch, there was, strictly speaking, a gap in the formal procedure of dharma-transmission—and for this reason, successive generations of the Sichuan lineage who held this robe of faith never dared to claim the title of "patriarch" (祖) for themselves. This in itself reflects something telling about the public understanding, within the High Tang Chan community, of the different sources of legitimacy carried by "the robe" versus "the mind-seal" respectively—providing another instructive point of comparison for the many later controversies over whether "transmitting the robe" is equivalent to "transmitting the Dharma" (a controversy that likewise occupies a central place in the narrative of Huineng's own "robe traveling south").

### 2. Wuxiang and the Jingzhong School: The Three-Phrase Teaching of "No Recollection, No Thought, Do Not Forget"

The most influential of Chuji's disciples was Wuxiang (無相), a prince of the Silla royal house (present-day Korean peninsula) by birth, lay surname Kim, popularly known among the people of Sichuan as "Monk Kim" (金和尚)—his precise birth and death dates remain disputed, with sources variously giving 684–762, 648–742, or 680–756, and no scholarly consensus has yet been reached. Wuxiang crossed the sea to China in the sixteenth year of the Kaiyuan era (728 CE); he was first received in audience by Emperor Xuanzong and registered at Chan Ding Monastery in Chang'an, before traveling west into Sichuan, where he became a disciple of Chuji at De Chun Monastery in Zizhou (present-day Zizhong, Sichuan), demonstrating his sincerity in seeking the Dharma by burning his own finger as a lamp-offering; Chuji thereupon accepted him as a disciple and bestowed on him the name "Wuxiang" ("No Characteristic"). Wuxiang presided over Jingzhong Monastery in Chengdu, hence the school he founded came to be called the "Jingzhong School." When Emperor Xuanzong fled to Sichuan during the An Lushan Rebellion, he treated Wuxiang with great reverence; the Chengdu county magistrate Yang Yi likewise raised funds on his behalf to build Jingzhong, Bodhi, and Ningguo monasteries, and Xuanzong subsequently commissioned the construction of the large-scale Da Sheng Ci Monastery (comprising ninety-six courtyards, with a thousand mu of land granted), ordering Wuxiang to "establish its regulations"—clear evidence of the exalted standing and abundant official patronage he enjoyed within Sichuan's Buddhist community for a time. This pattern of acquiring institutional advantage bears comparison, in its structure, with the historical circumstances discussed above in which Sengchou in the Northern Dynasties and Shenxiu in the High Tang each received imperial patronage.

Wuxiang's meditation teaching, as recorded in the Dunhuang manuscript *Lidai Fabao Ji* (歷代法寶記, "Record of the Dharma-Treasure Through the Generations"), centers on a three-phrase formula: "no recollection, no thought, do not forget" (無憶、無念、莫忘, in one variant reading, "do not err," 莫妄): "No recollection is precepts; no thought is concentration; not erring is wisdom—these three phrases together constitute the gate of the total-holding [dhāraṇī]"—directly correlating the traditional threefold training of precepts, concentration, and wisdom with a mind-nature theory of "no recollection, no thought," a highly condensed and economical formula for practice and realization. Intriguingly, Wuxiang himself claimed that his meditation teaching descended "directly from Bodhidharma," rather than being transmitted through the Zhishen-Chuji lineage—a self-positioning that in itself reveals a certain tension and fluidity in lineage identity within the Sichuan school. Wuxiang taught in Sichuan for some twenty years, during which "the flourishing of his Chan style spread throughout the whole of Yizhou [Sichuan]"; before his death he passed the robe of faith on to his disciple Wuzhu.

Western scholarship's systematic study of this Sichuan lineage owes its foundation above all to the American scholar Wendi Adamek: her book *The Mystique of Transmission* offers an in-depth examination of the historical context of the eighth-century Sichuan meditation community, and her subsequent *The Teachings of Master Wuzhu: Zen and Religion of No-Religion* provides a complete English translation of, and detailed introduction to, the *Lidai Fabao Ji*, situating Wuzhu's "anti-institutional" "religion of no-religion" within the broader context of medieval Chinese Buddhist intellectual history—and pointing out that the account of Empress Wu Zetian bestowing the robe is very likely a textual construction devised by the Baotang School to establish its own legitimacy (one that conflicts with the "accepted" belief of the time that the robe of faith was enshrined at Huineng's stūpa in Shaozhou). Adamek's work stands as the single most important piece of Western scholarship on the Baotang School to date.

### 3. Wuzhu and the Baotang School

Wuzhu (無住, 714–774) is said to have "resembled Wuxiang in appearance, and was regarded as Wuxiang's incarnation"; after studying under Wuxiang for a mere three days he had already "deeply penetrated the essentials of the teaching," becoming his dharma-heir. He subsequently resided at Baotang Monastery in Chengdu, hence the school he founded came to be called the "Baotang School." Wuzhu inherited and further developed Wuxiang's teaching of "no recollection, no thought, do not forget"; he too practiced ascetic discipline and was fond of the art of Chan tea. His recorded sayings (likewise preserved through the Dunhuang manuscript *Lidai Fabao Ji*) advocate "extinguishing consciousness unconstrained by either doctrine or practice"—a still more radically condensed and iconoclastic teaching than that of the Jingzhong School, marked by a considerable degree of "castigating the teachings and destroying [attachment to] characteristics."

There is a phenomenon here, rich in theoretical interest yet seldom fully discussed, that deserves particular emphasis: the dharma-names of the two successive patriarchs of the Jingzhong and Baotang schools happen to be "Wuxiang" (無相, "No Characteristic") and "Wuzhu" (無住, "No Abiding")—these two characters, together with "wunian" (無念, "no thought"), are almost precisely the famous "three-phrase formula" through which the later *Platform Sūtra* summarizes the fundamental tenet of Huineng's own meditation teaching: "In this Dharma-gate of mine, from ancient times, we first establish no-thought as the doctrine, no-characteristic as the substance, and no-abiding as the root!" Whether this striking terminological overlap represents purely coincidental resonance in the history of ideas (the Prajñāpāramitā "threefold negation" is, after all, common vocabulary across Mahāyāna scripture and treatise literature, and it would hardly be surprising for different Chan lineages to independently converge on such usage), or whether the compilation process of the *Platform Sūtra* did in fact, to some degree, absorb and blend in existing terminological resources from the Sichuan lineage, remains an intriguing puzzle within Chan philology that still awaits further discussion; this study leaves the question open without asserting a definitive conclusion, and simply calls it to the reader's attention.

### 4. Moheyan and the Tibetan "Debate of Sudden and Gradual": The Sichuan Lineage's Echo Beyond China's Borders

Better known to scholarship than the Sichuan lineage's own westward transmission into Tibet is the story of the Dunhuang Chan monk Moheyan (摩訶衍, biographical details unknown, active in the latter half of the eighth century), who, at the invitation of the Tibetan Tsenpo Trisong Detsen, entered Tibet via Shazhou in 786 to propagate Chan teaching. According to the Dunhuang Chinese-language manuscript *Dunwu Dasheng Zhengli Jue Bing Xu* (頓悟大乘正理決并序, "Bull's-Eye Decision on the Correct Principle of the Mahāyāna of Sudden Awakening, with Preface"; catalogued as Pelliot Chinois 4646 and others), Moheyan propagated "sudden awakening" Chan teaching at Lhasa, Changzhu (Tradruk Monastery), and Bamplé (Samye Monastery), and for a time "Chan flourished vigorously." Between 792 and 794, the Tibetan royal court convened a public debate between the Indian Madhyamaka master Kamalaśīla and Moheyan on which represented the true path—"sudden awakening" (advocated by Moheyan, who held that Buddhahood could be attained through "no thought, no contemplation" without need of graduated practice) or "gradual awakening" (advocated by the Indian monks, who held that graduated cultivation through the stages of accumulation and preparation was required)—an event known to history as the "Lhasa Debate" or the "Samye Debate" (referred to in Tibetan sources as the "Debate of Sudden and Gradual"). Tibetan-language sources record that this debate ultimately ended with Moheyan's concession of defeat, "presenting a garland of flowers" in acknowledgment; thereafter, the Indian Madhyamaka–gradualist line was established as the orthodox direction for Tibetan Buddhism. Yet the account preserved in the Chinese-language *Dunwu Dasheng Zhengli Jue*, representing the other side, indicates that Moheyan nonetheless continued to receive the honorific title of "Great Virtuous One" and a considerable degree of continued respect thereafter; Chan's influence in Tibet was likewise not abruptly and completely extinguished by this debate—the names of his disciples Namkhé Nyingpo and Yeshé Yang continue to appear frequently in rock inscriptions found across Qinghai, Gansu, and Tibet.

What deserves particular inclusion in the purview of this study is this: research by the Japanese scholars Yamaguchi Zuihō and Obata Hiroyuki has established that, even before Moheyan's entry into Tibet in 786, the Jingzhong School, led by "Monk Kim" Wuxiang, had already been transmitted into Tibet through some channel; and that a portion of Baotang-School founder Wuzhu's recorded sayings has likewise survived in Tibetan-language manuscript form (such as Pelliot tibétain 116). In other words, the "beyond-Huineng" Sichuan lineage was, in fact, the earliest branch of the Chinese Chan tradition—and one transmitted through a relatively independent channel—to enter the purview of Tibetan Buddhism; its historical significance is by no means inferior to that of the later, far more celebrated case of Moheyan's "Debate of Sudden and Gradual." Yet because the surviving textual record is comparatively scattered, and reconstruction depends on the specialized reading of Dunhuang Old Tibetan manuscripts, this episode has long failed to enter the general awareness of Chinese-language readers—which is precisely the reason this study singles it out for treatment in its own dedicated section, incorporating it into the map of "traditions beyond Huineng."

## Part Seven: The Tiantai Contemplative System

Flourishing at nearly the same time as the Northern School, the Oxhead School, and the Sichuan lineages discussed above—yet independent of the Southern School in an entirely different sense—and developing along a Prajñāpāramitā-Madhyamaka line of thought that owes nothing to either the Sarvāstivādin Abhidharma tradition or the tathāgatagarbha theory of the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*, is the "śamatha-vipaśyanā" (止觀, "calm and insight," hereafter "zhiguan") contemplative system that would become the core practice-discourse of the Tiantai school. The Tiantai patriarchs—Huiwen, Huisi, Zhiyi, Guanding, Zhiwei, Huiwei, Xuanlang, Zhanran, and others—are all referred to as "Chan masters" (禪師) in early sources such as the *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*, and were indeed renowned for their meditative practice; yet their lineage's origins and doctrinal foundations have no connection whatsoever to any branch of the Bodhidharma tradition—theirs is an entirely independently developed path of contemplative practice.

### 1. Lineage Origins: Nāgārjunian Madhyamaka Rather than Laṅkāvatāra Mind-Nature Theory

The Tiantai zhiguan teaching traces, in its own internal transmission-lineage tradition, the "succession of present-day teachers" as follows: Huiwen (慧文) of the Northern Qi (dates unknown, active roughly 534–577)—Nanyue Huisi (慧思, 515–577)—Tiantai Zhiyi (智顗, 538–598). According to Guanding's record in the *Mohe Zhiguan*, Huiwen, through reading Nāgārjuna's *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* passage "the three kinds of wisdom are attained within a single mind" and the verse from the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, "that which arises through dependent origination, I declare to be empty; it is also a provisional name, and also the meaning of the middle way," awakened to the principle of the threefold, mutually-perfect truth of emptiness, provisional existence, and the middle, thereby founding the method of "the threefold contemplation within a single mind" (一心三觀). Huisi inherited this meditative teaching and further fused it with the doctrine of the "ten suchnesses" (十如是) from the *Lotus Sūtra*, forming his own distinctive teachings of the "Lotus samādhi" and "the true reality of all dharmas," while promoting both concentration and wisdom equally. Zhiyi studied under Huisi and fully inherited his transmission; he subsequently taught at Jinling, Jingzhou, Mount Tiantai, and elsewhere during the Chen-Sui transition, formally establishing the Tiantai school, which takes "zhiguan" as its guiding thread and is renowned for its equal excellence in both doctrine and contemplative practice.

It is particularly worth emphasizing that this lineage's doctrinal origins trace directly back to Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra*—the Prajñāpāramitā-Madhyamaka system—an entirely different strand of Indian Mahāyāna thought from the tathāgatagarbha–ālayavijñāna-based path of "entering principle by means of the teaching" that the Bodhidharma lineage takes from the *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra*. Although the Tiantai school's own legend of "oral transmission from the Buddha's own mouth" likewise traces back to Śākyamuni, Kāśyapa, and Ānanda, and even to Aśvaghoṣa and Nāgārjuna, this legendary genealogy was woven independently of, and has no point of contact with, the Bodhidharma lineage's legendary genealogy of the "twenty-eight Indian patriarchs"; nor does one ever find, within the tradition, any claim that "the Tiantai patriarchs once studied under Bodhidharma." Master Taixu once stated explicitly that the "reality-contemplation Chan" tradition, beginning from Kumārajīva's translation of the meditation essentials, remained a matter of mere "transmission and recitation"; it was Huiwen, Huisi, and Zhiyi, the three patriarchs of Tiantai, who truly "put it into actual practice and formally established the Middle Way reality-contemplation Chan"—a statement that resonates precisely with this study's theme of "meditation systems developing independently of the various Bodhidharma-lineage traditions."

### 2. Zhiyi's Three Forms of Zhiguan: Gradual, Variable, and Perfect-Sudden

Zhiyi's teaching career was prolific, and his zhiguan-related writings alone constitute a self-contained system, later summarized as the "Three Great Works" (三大部) of Tiantai zhiguan literature, classified according to the order of practice and realization into three forms:

**Gradual zhiguan** (漸次止觀)—that is, the *Shi Chan Boluomi Cidi Famen* (釋禪波羅蜜次第法門, "Sequential Gates of the Perfection of Meditation Explained," ten fascicles, also known as the *Chanmen Xiuzheng*), which Zhiyi expounded in his earlier years and his disciple Fashen recorded; taking the term "chan" as encompassing the entirety of the Buddhadharma, it proceeds from shallow to deep in orderly sequence, like ascending a tower step by step, and is the largest in scope of the three forms of zhiguan, containing the most complete treatment of the concrete technical details of meditative practice.

**Variable zhiguan** (不定止觀)—that is, the *Liu Miao Famen* (六妙法門, "Six Wondrous Dharma-Gates," one fascicle), which Zhiyi expounded at Waguan Monastery in Jinling at the request of the Chief Minister Mao Xi, a work that took eight years to complete, from the first year of the Guangda era of the Chen dynasty (567) to the seventh year of the Taijian era (575); it expounds the "six wondrous gates" of counting, following, stopping, contemplating, returning, and purifying, which can be freely and flexibly applied at varying degrees of depth and suddenness or gradualness—like a gem of vajra flashing unpredictably in the sunlight—hence the name "variable."

**Perfect-sudden zhiguan** (圓頓止觀)—that is, the *Mohe Zhiguan* (摩訶止觀, "Great Calming and Contemplation," ten fascicles, recorded by Guanding), which Zhiyi expounded at Yuquan Monastery in Jingzhou; taking the deepest and most exalted state of mind as its direct point of departure, it unfolds Zhiyi's own fully mature system of contemplative practice, representing, in direct and forthright fashion, the most profound and essential of the three forms of zhiguan in its intent—it has traditionally been regarded as one of the most important foundational treatises of the Tiantai school.

Beyond these three, there is also a simplified introductory manual designed for beginners, the *Xiuxi Zhiguan Zuochan Fayao* (修習止觀坐禪法要, "Essentials of Cultivating Zhiguan and Seated Meditation," one fascicle, also known as the *Tongmeng Zhiguan* or *Xiao Zhiguan*, "Lesser Zhiguan"), which systematically introduces the twenty-five preliminary expedients, the counteraction of the five hindrances to the path, the ten essential points of formal zhiguan practice, and other content, in plain and clearly organized language—it has traditionally been regarded as one of the most important introductory textbooks for zhiguan practice in all of Chinese Buddhism, and its breadth and depth of influence has, if anything, exceeded that of the far more voluminous *Mohe Zhiguan* itself. In addition, Zhiyi's disciples recorded the *Chanmen Koujue* (禪門口訣, "Oral Instructions on the Gate of Chan"), a text devoted to breath-regulation-based methods of seated meditation for healing illness—"the circulation of qi in later martial arts," it has been observed, "traces its origins to the gate of Chan"—a fascinating instance of Tiantai contemplative practice spilling over into secular health-cultivation culture.

### 3. The "Twenty-Five Expedients" and the "Ten Vehicles of Contemplation"

Tiantai zhiguan literature is especially notable for its rigorous, step-by-step construction. Most representative are, first, the "twenty-five expedients" (二十五方便) set out in the *Xiao Zhiguan*—encompassing five major categories (fulfilling the five conditions; rejecting the five desires; abandoning the five hindrances; regulating the five matters of food, sleep, body, breath, and mind; and practicing the five methods of aspiration, diligence, mindfulness, skillful wisdom, and single-mindedness), totaling twenty-five items of foundational preparation prior to formal contemplative practice; and second, the "ten vehicles of contemplation" (十乘觀法) set out in the *Mohe Zhiguan* (contemplating the inconceivable object; arousing genuine bodhicitta; skillfully stabilizing zhiguan; refuting all dharmas comprehensively; discerning what obstructs and what does not; adjusting the aids to the path; employing counteragents to open the way; knowing one's stage; being able to endure; and being free of attachment even to the Dharma), which proceed by progressive stages to form a complete practical map of Tiantai "perfect-sudden zhiguan" from the initial arousal of the aspiration for enlightenment all the way to ultimate attainment. These two systems stand in sharp contrast to the economical style of the Bodhidharma–Huineng lineage, with its "no reliance on words, a special transmission outside the teachings," and fully embody Tiantai zhiguan's distinctive character of "equal excellence in doctrine and contemplation," of "understanding and practice each given their full due."

### 4. Transmission Beyond China's Borders: Japanese Tendai and Korean Cheontae

From the Tang dynasty onward, Tiantai zhiguan teaching spread across East Asia through two channels entirely independent of the eastward transmission of the Chan school itself (such as the transmission of Northern School Chan to Japan through Puji's disciple Dōsen, discussed above). First: the Japanese monk Saichō (最澄, 767–822) traveled to Tang China to seek the Dharma, receiving Tiantai contemplative teaching from Daosui and Xingman on Mount Tiantai; upon returning home he founded the Japanese Tendai school, whose very name and method of practice—"zhiguan," rendered in Japanese as *shikan*—derive directly from Tiantai China. His successors Ennin and Enchin subsequently likewise traveled to Tang China to seek the Dharma, propagating both Tiantai zhiguan and Esoteric Buddhist teachings together, giving rise to what later became known as the "Taimitsu" (台密, "Tendai Esotericism") lineage (as distinct from the "Tōmitsu," 東密, "Esotericism transmitted through Kūkai," discussed in the following section on Chinese Esoteric Buddhism's eastward transmission). Second: Uicheon (義天, 1055–1101), a Korean prince by birth, traveled to Song China to seek the Dharma, and upon returning home founded the "Cheontae" school (天台宗, the Korean-Goryeo equivalent of Tiantai) in Korea, likewise directly inheriting the Tiantai contemplation-and-doctrine system of China. Both cases exemplify "zhiguan" spreading and taking root independently across East Asia, quite apart from the Chan lineage—further demonstrating that as a complete system of contemplative practice, "zhiguan" possesses a historical vitality and sphere of influence in no way inferior to that of the Southern School, which would later come to overshadow it.

## Part Eight: Pure Land Meditation—From the Pratyutpanna Samādhi to the Sixteen Contemplations

In the common impression of later Chinese Buddhism, the Pure Land tradition is often associated with vocal-recitation buddha-name practice, and even framed within a binary opposition of "Chan practice and Pure Land practice" as though they were separate categories entirely—"Chan is Chan, Pure Land is Pure Land." Yet tracing the tradition back to its source, the original core practice of the Pure Land school was in essence a highly technical form of "Chan"—a contemplative method taking visualization as its central technique and samādhi-attainment as its direct goal, and one that, like the zhiguan and four-foundations-of-mindfulness systems discussed above, ought properly to be regarded as one of the independent meditation traditions existing beyond the Southern School.

### 1. The *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra* and Huiyuan's Practice of Buddha-Recollection Samādhi

The *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra* (般舟三昧經, also translated as the "Sūtra of the Buddha's Standing Presence") is one of the earliest scriptural bases for the buddha-recollection tradition, already translated in the Eastern Han by Lokakṣema (支婁迦讖). Its "pratyutpanna samādhi" method is extremely rigorous: over a period ranging from seven to ninety days, the practitioner walks continuously day and night without sitting or lying down, ceaselessly reciting Amitābha Buddha's name aloud while continuously visualizing the thirty-two major and eighty minor marks of Amitābha's form in the mind, thereby drawing the buddhas of the ten directions—Amitābha above all—to manifest in samādhi. What is distinctive about this scripture is that it does not stop at the mystical experience of "seeing the Buddha" as an end in itself, but goes further, requiring the practitioner to turn contemplation back upon their own mind and realize that the buddhas thus seen "are all produced by the mind's own workings, illusory transformations," their "nature originally empty and quiescent"—thereby ultimately directing buddha-recollection samādhi toward the realization of Prajñāpāramitā emptiness. This makes the practice transmitted in the *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra* markedly different from the later historical trajectory in which vocal-recitation buddha-name practice gradually became independent of contemplative practice and doctrinal understanding, and shows it to bear a far closer inner connection to Prajñāpāramitā-Madhyamaka thought.

As discussed above, Huiyuan of Mount Lu was indeed "the first figure in Chinese history to systematically propagate this Dharma-gate," and his practice "took meditative wisdom as its foundation"—visualization buddha-recollection rather than vocal-recitation buddha-recollection—amply demonstrating that, in its formative stage, Pure Land buddha-recollection was in fact a branch of contemplative practice, not a separate "school" standing in opposition to it. History records no shortage of practitioners who have undertaken the extraordinarily demanding standing-practice method described in the *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra*: Elder Miaolian of Lingyan Mountain Monastery in Taiwan is said to have completed the full ninety-day pratyutpanna-samādhi practice on multiple occasions, and a well-known legend of a spiritual response is also associated with Eikan, seventh abbot of Eikandō in Kyoto, Japan—showing that although this ancient Dharma-gate is extraordinarily difficult to undertake and has been practiced by relatively few over the centuries, it has never once been entirely broken off.

### 2. The "Sixteen Contemplations" of the *Contemplation of Amitāyus Sūtra*

Complementary to, and even more influential than, the *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra* in shaping later Pure Land visualization practice is the *Sūtra on the Contemplation of Amitāyus Buddha* (佛說觀無量壽佛經, the "Contemplation Sūtra"), translated by Kālayaśas of the Liu Song. Marked in its very title by the character "contemplation" (觀), this scripture systematically teaches sixteen progressively sequenced methods of visualization (the "sixteen contemplations," 十六觀), beginning with foundational visualizations of the sun, water, and ground, then proceeding to visualize the adorned dependent-world features of the Pure Land—jeweled trees, jeweled ponds, jeweled towers and pavilions—and further to visualize the adorned major and minor marks of Amitābha Buddha and the bodhisattvas Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta, concluding with the contemplation of the nine grades of rebirth across the three ranks of upper, middle, and lower (this being precisely the scriptural basis for the "nine-grades-of-rebirth" Amitābha Pure Land transformation tableau carved on the west wall of the Central Cave at Xiaonanhai, discussed above). The scripture's use of the term "mind's eye" (心眼) makes explicit that this capacity for visualization "is able, through the power of meditative concentration, to see through to forms beyond ordinary obstruction, illuminating all dharmas"—that is, it is achieved not through the physical eye or the divine eye, but through a special capacity for contemplative vision accomplished by the power of meditative concentration; its character as a form of contemplative practice is beyond dispute.

### 3. From Tanluan to Shandao: The Systematization and Transformation of Pure Land Contemplative Method

Between Huiyuan and Shandao (善導, 613–681), Tanluan (曇鸞, 476–542, some accounts giving a date after 554) of the Northern Wei stands as a crucial transitional figure in the evolution of Pure Land thought. In his youth he read widely and delved deeply into Prajñāpāramitā-Madhyamaka thought and Buddha-nature theory; falling ill while writing a commentary on the *Mahāsaṃnipāta Sūtra*, whose profound meaning he found difficult to grasp, he traveled south in search of the Daoist scholar Tao Hongjing to seek methods of longevity, receiving ten fascicles of Daoist scriptures on immortality-practice. On his return journey he chanced to meet Bodhiruci at Luoyang, who bestowed upon him the *Contemplation of Amitāyus Sūtra*; tradition holds that Tanluan thereupon turned to dedicated Pure Land practice. His *Wangsheng Lun Zhu* (往生論注, "Commentary on the Treatise on Rebirth [in the Pure Land]") exerted a profound influence on later Pure Land thought, and stands as an important precursor to the systematized Pure Land thought later developed by Daochuo and Shandao.

In the Tang dynasty, Shandao composed the *Guan Jing Si Tie Shu* (觀經四帖疏, "Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra in Four Fascicles"), providing a systematic exegesis of the *Contemplation Sūtra*. From this point, Pure Land buddha-recollection practice gradually developed and simplified—moving from an early contemplative system centered on visualization samādhi toward one centered primarily on vocal-recitation buddha-name practice, supplemented by visualization—and eventually, from the Song-Yuan period onward, stood alongside the Southern School of Chan as one of the two most socially influential traditions of practice in Chinese Buddhism. Yet as this section has shown, tracing the Pure Land tradition back to its source reveals that its underlying "samādhi" character, and its original orientation of "taking meditative wisdom as its foundation," demonstrate precisely that, methodologically speaking, it belongs—just as the various branches of the Bodhidharma lineage do—to the broader family of "Chan" as one of its independently developed members, rather than to some separate category conventionally understood as standing opposed to "Chan."

## Part Nine: Yogācāra Contemplation—The Yogācāra School and the "Five-Fold Vijñaptimātratā Contemplation"

The very name of the "Yogācāra school" (瑜伽行派, Yogācāra, literally "practitioners of yoga") derives directly from the Sanskrit *yoga* ("union," "meditative discipline")—a naming that itself demonstrates that, from its Indian origins, Vijñaptimātratā ("consciousness-only," Weishi 唯識) doctrine bore an essential, inherent connection to contemplative practice. Its foundational treatise, the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* (瑜伽師地論, traditionally attributed to Maitreya as expounded and Asaṅga as recorder; fully translated into Chinese by Xuanzang in one hundred fascicles), devotes an entire dedicated section (fascicles 26–34) to expounding the stages of zhiguan practice—clear evidence that zhiguan practice is an inseparable component of Yogācāra Vijñaptimātratā theory as a whole, rather than merely a purely speculative system of consciousness-analysis.

After its transmission to China, Yogācāra Vijñaptimātratā thought passed through three stages of development and differentiation: the Dilun masters (地論師, the "true-mind" branch, which merged with tathāgatagarbha thought), the Shelun masters (攝論師, transmitted by Paramārtha, representing a transitional position between the "false-mind" and "true-mind" branches), and finally the Faxiang Vijñaptimātratā school (法相唯識宗, the "false-mind" branch in the strict sense) established by Xuanzang and his disciple Kuiji. Of these, the figure who truly transformed Vijñaptimātratā doctrine into a concrete, practicable system of contemplative method was Kuiji (窺基, 632–682), a disciple of Xuanzang. In his representative work, the "Chapter on Vijñaptimātratā" within the *Dasheng Fayuan Yilin Zhang* (大乘法苑義林章), Kuiji drew on the doctrinal framework of "progressively entering, in sequence, the imagined, dependent, and perfected natures" (遍計所執、依他起、圓成實三性次第悟入) found in the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s "Chapter on Entering the Characteristics of the Knowable," gathering material from the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra*, the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*, the *Vijñaptimātratāsiddhi-śāstra*, and other scriptures and treatises, to organize the "five-fold vijñaptimātratā contemplation" (五重唯識觀)—a method of practice and realization distinctive to the Vijñaptimātratā school:

1. **Setting aside the false, preserving the real** (遣虛存實識, contrasting emptiness and existence): setting aside the illusory imagined nature, preserving the real dependent and perfected natures;
2. **Discarding the extraneous, retaining the pure** (捨濫留純識, contrasting subject and object): since no dharma exists apart from consciousness, and consciousness divides into mind and its objects, one discards the objective aspect internal to the mind, retaining only the eight consciousnesses and fifty-one mental factors that constitute the subjective, cognizing aspect;
3. **Gathering the branches back to the root** (攝末歸本識, contrasting essence and function): mind and mental factors each possess a self-verifying aspect (essence) as well as an objective and a subjective aspect (function); one gathers the function of the objective and subjective aspects back into the essence of self-verification;
4. **Concealing the inferior, revealing the superior** (隱劣顯勝識, contrasting ruler and retinue): the mind-king is superior, the mental factors inferior; one gathers the inferior back into the superior;
5. **Setting aside phenomena, realizing the nature** (遣相證性識, contrasting phenomena and principle): one sets aside the phenomenal aspect of the dependent nature, seeking realization solely in terms of the noumenal principle of the perfected nature—the fundamental wisdom first realizes suchness, and the subsequently attained wisdom then comprehends the dependent nature, whereupon the two forms of attachment, to self and to dharmas, naturally cease.

The first four stages observe the principle of vijñaptimātratā progressively, layer by layer, in terms of the characteristics of the dependent nature; the fifth stage contemplates the wondrous principle of vijñaptimātratā directly, in terms of the nature of the perfected reality—proceeding from coarse to subtle, from shallow to deep, ultimately arriving at the state of "the fusion of principle and wisdom." Though economical in its formulation, this method of contemplation stands as the most important concrete legacy of practical soteriology bequeathed to later generations by the Tang-dynasty Faxiang Vijñaptimātratā school—developing independently of, and without any point of contact with, the Tiantai zhiguan system that arose in the same era, or the various branches of Bodhidharma-lineage meditation discussed in Parts Three through Six of this study—together constituting one strand within the pluralistic, coexisting landscape of contemplative traditions under the Sui-Tang Buddhist paradigm of "according equal weight to doctrine and contemplation."

## Part Ten: The Huayan Dharmadhātu Contemplation—Another Perfect-Teaching System of Contemplative Practice Independent of the Chan Lineage

Like Tiantai zhiguan and the five-fold vijñaptimātratā contemplation, the Huayan (華嚴) school likewise developed a system of contemplative practice entirely independent of the Bodhidharma-lineage genealogy of meditation—one that nonetheless occupies an exalted place in the history of Chinese Buddhist thought: the "Dharmadhātu contemplation" (法界觀). Its founder, Dushun, is unusual even among the founders of Chinese Buddhist schools for having been revered by later generations as an incarnation of Mañjuśrī Bodhisattva—a founding figure distinguished by direct practical realization rather than by scholastic argumentation.

### 1. Dushun and the *Fajie Guan Men*: The Three Dharmadhātu Contemplations

Dushun (杜順, 557–640, First Patriarch of the Huayan school; lay surname Du, dharma-name Fashun, granted the honorific title "Dixin," "Imperial Heart"), left home at Yinsheng Monastery at age eighteen, "devoting himself entirely to meditative contemplation." Tradition credits him with numerous miraculous deeds—"curing deafness and muteness, driving away insects, conversing with cattle and horses"—and Emperor Wen of Sui held him in great esteem; his contemporaries called him the "Bodhisattva of Dunhuang." Dushun authored two works that laid the foundation for Huayan contemplative theory: the *Wujiao Zhiguan* (五教止觀, "Zhiguan of the Five Teachings") and the *Fajie Guan Men* (法界觀門, "Gate of Dharmadhātu Contemplation")—later Huayan students have traditionally judged "whether one truly carries forward the lamp of the Huayan tradition" precisely by whether one has thoroughly assimilated these two texts, testament to their weighty standing.

The "three Dharmadhātu contemplations" (法界三觀) set out in the *Fajie Guan Men* are: the **contemplation of true emptiness** (真空觀, also called the "contemplation of true emptiness, transcending characteristics"), grounded in the Dharmadhātu of principle, contemplating that the intrinsic nature of all dharmas is empty, thereby dispelling attachment to both the extremes of "emptiness" and "existence"; the **contemplation of the unimpeded interpenetration of principle and phenomena** (理事無礙觀), contemplating that "principle" (true suchness, emptiness) and "phenomena" (the manifest world of appearances) are mutually identical and mutually interpenetrating, without obstruction; and the **contemplation of the unimpeded interpenetration and mutual inclusion of all phenomena** (周遍含容觀), which goes further still, contemplating that "phenomena" and "phenomena" are likewise mutually identical and mutually interpenetrating, layer upon layer without end—this is precisely the hallmark Huayan state of "the unimpeded interpenetration of phenomenon and phenomenon" (事事無礙), the most profound and consummate of Dushun's three contemplations.

The *Wujiao Zhiguan*, in turn, follows the Huayan school's distinctive five-stage doctrinal classification—the teachings of the Hīnayāna, the initial Mahāyāna, the final Mahāyāna, the sudden Mahāyāna, and the perfect One Vehicle—assigning to each a corresponding method of zhiguan practice (the gate of dharmas existing but no self; the gate of arising that is itself non-arising; the gate of the perfect fusion of phenomena and principle; the gate of the simultaneous transcendence of speech and contemplation; and the gate of the Huayan samādhi), proceeding step by step from shallow to deep in a manner that ultimately returns to the fundamental contemplative state of "the unimpeded interpenetration of phenomenon and phenomenon" central to the Huayan perfect teaching—a conceptual design bearing no small resemblance to Zhiyi's classification of the "three forms of zhiguan" according to stages of practice and realization, discussed above, and yet entirely independent, belonging solely to the Huayan school's own doctrinal system.

### 2. Zhiyan's Ten Mysterious Gates, and the Theoretical Systematization Achieved by Fazang and Chengguan

The Second Patriarch, Zhiyan (智儼), building on Dushun's three contemplations, further developed the "ten mysterious gates" (十玄門—including, among its ten items, the gate of simultaneous correspondence and completeness, and the gate of the unimpeded mutual freedom of the broad and the narrow, and the gate of the mutual containment of the one and the many without difference), setting out in concrete terms the ten distinct facets internally displayed within "the Dharmadhātu of the unimpeded interpenetration of phenomenon and phenomenon." The Third Patriarch, Fazang (法藏, the figure who in effect brought Huayan doctrine to its full systematic culmination), building further on the three contemplations, formally introduced the term "the four Dharmadhātus" (四法界): the Dharmadhātu of phenomena, the Dharmadhātu of principle, the Dharmadhātu of the unimpeded interpenetration of principle and phenomena, and the Dharmadhātu of the unimpeded interpenetration of phenomenon and phenomenon—theorizing Dushun's "three contemplations" into a complete ontological framework. The Fourth Patriarch, Chengguan (澄觀), composed the *Huayan Fajie Xuanjing* (華嚴法界玄鏡, "Mysterious Mirror of the Huayan Dharmadhātu"), a commentary on Dushun's *Fajie Guan Men*, ultimately systematizing and fixing the "four Dharmadhātus" framework into the standard formulation of Huayan contemplative theory still followed today; Guifeng Zongmi (the Fifth Patriarch, repeatedly cited in this study's discussion of the Oxhead School above) worked within this same framework as well, attempting to use the theory of "the four Dharmadhātus" to reconcile Chan and doctrinal teaching, harmonizing the Heze lineage with Huayan doctrine.

The Huayan Dharmadhātu contemplation, together with Tiantai zhiguan and the five-fold vijñaptimātratā contemplation, together constitute the three independently developed contemplative systems of the three great "doctrinal" schools of the Sui-Tang period—Tiantai, Huayan, and Faxiang Vijñaptimātratā. Although all three share a common origin in Indian Mahāyāna scripture and treatise literature, their specific doctrinal orientations differ markedly: Huayan grounds itself in the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s "dependent origination of the Dharmadhātu"; Vijñaptimātratā grounds itself in the *Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra* and the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*'s "the ten thousand dharmas are consciousness alone"; and Tiantai grounds itself in the *Lotus Sūtra* and the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra*'s "the true reality of all dharmas." Yet at the methodological level, all three display a path of "contemplating the mind by means of the teachings" markedly different from the Bodhidharma lineage's sudden-awakening approach of "no reliance on words, directly pointing to the mind": each remains grounded, at every stage, in the systematic construction of scriptural doctrine, guiding the practitioner through progressively deepening stages of contemplation to enter into the ultimate reality as each school's own doctrine understands it—and this, too, is a shared feature of considerable weight within the map of "traditions beyond Huineng's Southern School" that this study surveys.

## Part Eleven: Esoteric Buddhist Meditation (I)—The "Three Mysteries in Correspondence" of Chinese Esoteric Buddhism

Esoteric Buddhist (Vajrayāna, Mantrayāna) contemplative practice represents, beyond the Southern School, both the weightiest and the most intricately elaborate strand of all. Its Chinese and Tibetan branches, though sharing a common origin in late Indian Mahāyāna–Vajrayāna Buddhism, developed historically independently of one another; this study treats them in two separate parts, beginning with the Chinese ("Tangmi," 唐密, "Tang Esotericism") system.

In the eighth century, during the Kaiyuan era of Emperor Xuanzong of Tang (716–720), the Indian Esoteric masters Śubhakarasiṃha, Vajrabodhi, and Amoghavajra arrived successively in China; under the patronage of the Tang imperial house they systematically translated a large body of Esoteric scriptures and propagated Esoteric teaching at Da Xingshan Monastery in Chang'an and elsewhere—an event known to history as the arrival of the "Three Great Masters of the Kaiyuan Era" (開元三大士), marking the formal establishment of "Tang Esotericism," centered on the two "Pure" Esoteric systems of the Vajra-Realm and Womb-Realm maṇḍalas (this followed an earlier period in which only scattered "miscellaneous Esoteric" elements had been introduced, without yet forming a systematic body of doctrine and ritual). Śubhakarasiṃha, assisted by Yixing, translated the *Mahāvairocana Sūtra* (大日經) at Da Fuxian Monastery in Luoyang, marking the beginning of the Womb-Realm Esoteric teaching's transmission to China; Vajrabodhi subsequently translated the *Jingangding Yuqie Zhonglüe Chu Niansong Fa* (金剛頂瑜伽中略出念誦法) and other texts, transmitting the Vajra-Realm teaching; his disciple Amoghavajra, having received the empowerments of both maṇḍala systems from the ācārya Samantabhadra in Siṃhala (present-day Sri Lanka), returned to China and translated the *Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha* (Jin'gangding Jing 金剛頂經) and ten other works totaling 143 fascicles—Tang Esotericism thereby reached its full flowering, its patriarchal seats at Da Xingshan and Qinglong Monasteries flourishing greatly.

The doctrine of Tang Esotericism centers on the goal of "becoming Buddha in this very body" (即身成佛): through the practitioner's cultivation of the "three mysteries" (三密)—forming mudrās with the hands (body-mystery), reciting mantras/dhāraṇīs with the mouth (speech-mystery), and visualizing the tutelary deity with the mind (mind-mystery)—the practitioner enters into correspondence with the "three mysteries" of the tutelary buddha or bodhisattva's own body, speech, and mind ("the correspondence of the three mysteries," "empowerment through the three mysteries"), thereby transforming the ordinary body into the buddha-body and realizing buddhahood in this very lifetime, rather than requiring the immeasurable eons of gradual cultivation demanded by the exoteric teachings. This method of "correspondence of the three mysteries" rests doctrinally on two central theses: "dependent origination of the six great elements" (六大緣起, the six elements of earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness pervade the entire Dharma-realm, mutually unobstructed) and "the letter A is fundamentally unborn" (阿字本不生, the letter A is the root of all letters and all dharmas, and its very nature—"fundamentally unborn"—symbolizes how all dharmas are, in their essential nature, empty and quiescent while yet arising through dependent origination). Practice must take maṇḍala (altar-realm) visualization as its structural framework, proceeding through the sequential unfolding of empowerment, homa fire rituals, and other prescribed rites, with the three phrases "bodhicitta as cause, great compassion as root, skillful means as consummation" running through the entire course of practice and realization.

Following Emperor Wuzong's suppression of Buddhism during the Huichang era (845), the two Pure Esoteric systems of the Vajra-Realm and Womb-Realm largely disappeared from China proper; they survived only because Japanese monks who had earlier traveled to Tang China to seek the Dharma—Saichō, Kūkai, and others—had already transplanted the teaching intact to Japan, where it gave rise to what later became known as the "Tōmitsu" lineage (東密, "Esotericism" as transmitted by Kūkai's Shingon school) and the "Taimitsu" lineage (台密, the branch of the Tendai school that also propagated Esoteric teaching, transmitted by Saichō and his successors Ennin and Enchin, discussed above)—two traditions that continue to this day. In China proper, only certain practices of a "miscellaneous Esoteric" character (such as the Mengshan rite of feeding hungry ghosts, and the Flaming-Mouth rite) survived among the folk tradition, no longer regarded as an independent school. The severance of Tang Esotericism's transmission in its land of origin, alongside its complete preservation and continued flourishing in Japan, together constitute a rather dramatic episode of transmission-rupture and displaced continuation in the history of Chinese Buddhism—and form essential background for understanding today's East Asian Esoteric landscape (Tang Esotericism's disappearance from its native soil; the flourishing of Tōmitsu and Taimitsu overseas; and the independent development of Tibetan Esoteric Buddhism).

## Part Twelve: Esoteric Buddhist Meditation (II)—Tibetan Buddhism's Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen

Unlike Tang Esotericism, which was transmitted directly to China proper by sea and land from India and then further east to Japan, Tibetan Buddhist Esoteric teaching (Vajrayāna) underwent a far more prolonged and complex process of transmission, through the "early diffusion" and "later diffusion" periods, giving rise to the Nyingma (old-translation), Kagyu, Sakya, and Gelug (new-translation) schools, among others. Within Tibetan Buddhism, the two most representative systems for "the path of liberation focused on the nature of mind" are Mahāmudrā, of the new-translation schools, and Dzogchen, of the old-translation school—the two traditions often spoken of together as the "two main currents" of Tibetan Buddhist contemplative teaching.

### 1. Mahāmudrā: The Path of Liberation in the Kagyu School

"Mahāmudrā" is a translation of the Sanskrit term, in which "mahā" ("great") refers to the single ultimate source pervading all phenomena and all movement, while "mudrā" ("seal") suggests a gesture, symbol, or seal of confirmation—the compound term thus denoting a method of practice and realization in which ultimate wisdom "seals" or confirms that all phenomena, the entire universe, share a single, uniform taste. The Mahāmudrā teaching is traditionally traced back to the Indian mahāsiddha Saraha (active around the first century BCE), transmitted orally through Nāgārjuna and successive generations of masters down to Nāropa, who transmitted it to Marpa, founding patriarch of the Tibetan Kagyu school; upon returning to Tibet, Marpa transmitted it to Milarepa, who transmitted it to Gampopa—it was Gampopa who first combined the Mahāmudrā teachings with the "stages of the path" (Lamrim) pedagogical method of the Kadam school, systematizing them and thereby laying the basic framework for the Kagyu school's transmission of Mahāmudrā.

Mahāmudrā is customarily divided, according to the differing approaches by which it may be entered into practice, into three types: **Sūtra-based Mahāmudrā** (also called Exoteric Mahāmudrā), grounded in the Madhyamaka view and the thought of the extrinsic-emptiness tathāgatagarbha, drawing primarily on the *Samādhirāja Sūtra*, the *Ratnagotravibhāga*, and other scriptures and treatises; **Tantra-based Mahāmudrā**, which requires tantric empowerment and proceeds through the sequential introduction of the generation stage (visualizing a tutelary deity, such as Vajravārāhī or Cakrasaṃvara) and the completion stage (cultivation of the subtle winds, channels, and drops—among which the "Six Yogas of Nāropa" constitute the most essential completion-stage teaching within the Kagyu school); and **Essence Mahāmudrā**, in which the guru directly transmits pointing-out instructions on body, speech, and mind to the disciple in person, "mind sealing mind," directly introducing the disciple to the very essence of the nature of mind—Tilopa's instruction to Nāropa (the celebrated "Ganges Mahāmudrā") represents the classic example of this type of teaching. The Kagyu school's system of practice treats "the tantric path and the path of Mahāmudrā" as a unified whole: Sūtra-based Mahāmudrā's view of emptiness, drawn from the Madhyamaka tradition, is fused with the great bliss generated through the tantric path of skillful means, and through this fusion the practitioner directly realizes the three bodies—dharmakāya, saṃbhogakāya, and nirmāṇakāya—which stands as the single most distinctive hallmark differentiating the Kagyu school from the other schools of Tibetan Buddhism.

### 2. Dzogchen: The Atiyoga of the Nyingma School

Dzogchen (a transliteration of the Tibetan for "Great Perfection," also translated as Atiyoga, "the utmost yoga") is the highest of the three inner tantric vehicles within the "nine vehicles" doctrinal classification system of the Nyingma (old-translation) school, regarded as the very pinnacle of the entire Buddhadharma and of all tantric teaching. According to Nyingma tradition, Dzogchen teaching originates with the dharmakāya buddha Samantabhadra, transmitted through the saṃbhogakāya Vajrasattva to the nirmāṇakāya Garab Dorje (active roughly in the seventh–eighth century, a figure from Oḍḍiyāna in northwestern India, generally regarded by scholarship as the earliest credible human patriarch of the lineage), who transmitted it to Mañjuśrīmitra and Śrī Siṃha, and thence, through Padmasambhava, Vimalamitra, and others, into Tibet in the eighth century. Although many Dzogchen scriptures have reappeared in the form of "terma" (rediscovered treasure-texts), it is believed that a considerable portion were genuinely translated from Sanskrit originals rather than composed by Tibetans themselves; Longchenpa (1308–1364, honored as the "Omniscient Dharma-King") is generally regarded as the figure who brought the Dzogchen doctrinal system to its full culmination.

Dzogchen teaching is divided by content into three classes: the Mind Series, the Space Series, and the Instruction Series (also called the Esoteric Instruction Series), with the Instruction Series regarded as the highest; the Instruction Series is in turn divided, according to method of practice and realization, into two branches: **Trekchö** ("cutting through," "breakthrough") and **Thögal** ("leaping over," "direct crossing"), with the latter building upon the former as its foundation. Trekchö takes as its goal the realization of Dzogchen's fundamental doctrine that "the essence of mind is originally pure"; although called "cutting through," actual practice still generally requires a considerable degree of gradual cultivation before the practitioner can suddenly awaken to this originally pure essence of mind. Thögal, by contrast, is regarded as a still higher-level practice—"realizing the innate wisdom of dharmatā [ultimate reality] through the medium of light"—capable of achieving, within this very life, the "rainbow body" (or "great transference body," "vase body of youth"), attaining vast and unimpeded powers for benefiting sentient beings, along with such self-mastered supernormal abilities as the divine eye, unobstructed movement, and control over one's own lifespan—for which reason it is ranked among all Dzogchen teachings as the most ultimate and consummate form of practice.

Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen, as the "two main currents" of the mind-nature-focused path of liberation within Tibetan Buddhism, have been the subject of extensive comparative discussion by successive generations of masters within their own respective traditions—both, for instance, emphasize the coexistence of two distinct pedagogical paths: an "uncommon teaching" suited to practitioners of a particular capacity (directly pointing out the nature of mind, employing "ordinary mind" without contrived cultivation) and a "common teaching" applicable to all practitioners (the gradual development of calm-abiding and special insight, and the discernment of the two forms of selflessness through reasoned analysis)—and both traditions caution later students against neglecting the foundational, graduated teachings in favor of an empty, abstract discourse of "direct pointing-out." This kind of internal self-reflection in itself reflects the rich doctrinal self-awareness and accumulated experience of practice and realization within the Tibetan Esoteric tradition of mind-nature theory—a degree of subtlety and complexity in no way inferior to that of Chinese Southern-School Chan—and the two traditions have historically had little in the way of direct lineage contact with one another.

## Part Thirteen: Theravāda Śamatha-Vipassanā—From the Origin of "Jhāna" to the Twentieth-Century Vipassanā Revival

If one wishes, across the full range of Buddhist traditions, to trace "Chan" back to its most ancient root, one must return to the Pāli scriptural system on which Theravāda Buddhism is based—for "jhāna" (Sanskrit *dhyāna*) is itself precisely the term from which the Chinese character "chan" (禪) derives as a transliteration, and the four form-realm jhānas together with the four formless attainments (jointly, the "four jhānas and eight samādhis," 四禪八定) constitute the shared psychological foundation underlying every method of meditative practice within the Buddha's teaching—a foundation from which no form of Buddhist "Chan," from original Buddhism all the way down to the Southern School's sudden-awakening teaching, can, in principle, be considered independent.

### 1. Śamatha and Vipassanā

Theravāda Buddhism divides the whole of practice into two categories: **śamatha** (奢摩他, "calm-abiding," or "stopping," 止) and **vipassanā** (毗婆舍那, "insight," or "contemplation," 觀)—together, "śamatha-vipassanā"—concepts that overlap in meaning to a considerable degree with, yet also differ subtly in doctrine from, the identically-named concepts found in the Mahāyāna zhiguan systems of Tiantai and Vijñaptimātratā discussed above. The practitioner of śamatha fixes the mind single-pointedly upon a chosen object of meditation (a "meditation subject," *kammaṭṭhāna*), gradually entering "absorption concentration" (appanā) and progressively attaining the four jhānas in sequence; the practitioner of vipassanā, by contrast, uses wisdom to contemplate the true nature of all phenomena as impermanent, suffering, and without self, ultimately giving rise to liberating wisdom. Modern Theravāda Buddhism, particularly as represented by the Burmese meditation lineages, further distinguishes practitioners, according to their manner of undertaking śamatha-vipassanā practice, into "samatha-yānika" (samatha-vehicle practitioners, who cultivate deep concentration first before turning to insight practice) and "vipassanā-yānika" or "sukkha-vipassaka" ("dry-insight," or "pure-insight" practitioners, who bypass deep concentration and proceed directly to insight practice through a simplified approach)—it is this latter approach that forms the methodological basis on which the twentieth-century Burmese vipassanā revival movement was able to spread so widely, without requiring practitioners to meet the demanding preconditions of long-term monastic seclusion.

### 2. The Twentieth-Century Burmese Vipassanā Revival: Ledi Sayadaw, Mahasi Sayadaw, U Ba Khin, and Goenka

The Theravāda śamatha-vipassanā tradition has continued unbroken for over two thousand years; from the late nineteenth into the early twentieth century, under British colonial rule, Burma underwent a far-reaching modern revival movement whose social and cultural significance lay not only in technical innovations to the meditation method itself, but, more fundamentally, in the transformation of what had previously been an esoteric practice tradition confined to the monastic order into a "universal" method of body-mind training that could be widely practiced by lay society at large, and that, to a considerable degree, could be detached from any specific religious identity—ultimately becoming, in the latter half of the twentieth century, the shared point of origin for two movements that would sweep the globe: "Vipassana" and "Mindfulness."

**Ledi Sayadaw** (1846–1923) is universally recognized as the founder of this revival movement; in the early twentieth century he carried out a systematic reform of traditional insight-meditation methods, making them accessible to a far broader range of lay practitioners—an effort that was itself part of the Burmese people's cultural resistance to British colonial influence and their broader movement of indigenous Buddhist revival. Ledi Sayadaw's lineage of transmission runs: Saya Thetgyi—Sayagyi U Ba Khin (auditor-general of the Burmese government, a lay practitioner)—**S.N. Goenka** (1924–2013). From 1969 onward, Goenka "returned" this insight-meditation teaching to his ancestral homeland of India, subsequently spreading it widely to Europe, the Americas, Japan, Australia, Thailand, Nepal, and elsewhere, developing a standardized ten-day course format open to the general public regardless of religious background—today one of the most widely propagated systems of Theravāda meditation practice in the world.

Developing in parallel, and of no less profound an influence, is the lineage of **Mahasi Sayadaw** (1904–1982), which takes the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* as its fundamental scriptural basis and establishes a system of insight meditation centered on the core technique of "noting" present-moment phenomena of body and mind. In 1945, after seven months of sustained effort, he completed his monumental Burmese-language work, the *Manual of Insight* (an English translation was not fully published until 2016, by Wisdom Publications); after the war he propagated this method widely from the Buddhasāsana Yeiktha center in Rangoon, and his disciples spread throughout Southeast Asia and subsequently to Europe and the Americas—including Jack Kornfield and Joseph Goldstein, the important Western Dharma-teachers who founded the Insight Meditation Society in the United States, both of whom trace their own lineage directly or indirectly back to this tradition.

### 3. The Rise of the Mindfulness Movement and Its Relationship to Theravāda Insight Meditation

Through the "digestion and translation" carried out by Kornfield, Goldstein, and other American practitioners trained in the Mahasi insight tradition, this lineage gradually developed a mode of transmission adapted to the intellectual and cultural sensibilities of Western society. At the same time, another American scholar deeply influenced by meditative practice, Jon Kabat-Zinn, forged a path that went a step further still—one of "de-religionization" and "clinicalization"—giving rise to what is discussed below as the "Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction" (MBSR) movement, whose relationship to the Theravāda śamatha-vipassanā tradition—intricately interwoven, yet not without its own tensions—deserves separate and detailed treatment in the final part of this study.

## Part Fourteen: A Comparative Perspective—Indigenous Chinese Traditions of Quiet Contemplation (Non-Buddhist)

As a necessary supplement to this study, and in keeping with the comparative cross-religious perspective this author has consistently maintained, this part briefly examines two traditions that, although not Buddhist, interacted deeply with Buddhist meditation over the course of a long history, and are sometimes loosely grouped together in Chinese-language sources under similar terms such as "quiet sitting": the Daoist tradition of "fasting the mind" and "sitting in forgetfulness," and the quiet-sitting tradition of Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism. Strictly speaking, neither properly falls within the category of "Chan" (*dhyāna*) in its Buddhist sense, and so both are treated here only as comparative, background material, rather than being placed alongside the various Buddhist meditation systems discussed above as chapters of equal weight in the main body of this study.

### 1. Zhuangzi's "Fasting the Mind" and "Sitting in Forgetfulness"

Two celebrated parables in the Inner Chapters of the *Zhuangzi*, each cast in the form of a dialogue between Confucius and Yan Hui, have traditionally been regarded as the fountainhead texts of the Daoist tradition of contemplative self-cultivation. In the chapter "In the World of Men" (人間世), Confucius is made to expound "fasting the mind" (心齋): "Unify your intention. Do not listen with your ears, but listen with your mind. Do not listen with your mind, but listen with your qi. Hearing stops at the ears; the mind stops at what tallies with it. But qi is empty, and waits on all things. The Way gathers in emptiness alone. Emptiness—this is the fasting of the mind"—that is, one sets aside the discriminating grasp of both the senses and the intellect, allowing the spirit to become empty, quiescent, and unified, and, in this state of emptiness that "waits upon" things, one becomes attuned to the Way itself; this state of empty, quiescent clarity is precisely what is meant by "fasting the mind." In the chapter "The Great and Venerable Teacher" (大宗師), Yan Hui is made to describe, in progressively deepening stages, the attainment of "sitting in forgetfulness" (坐忘): first "forgetting benevolence and righteousness," then "forgetting rites and music," and finally "casting off the limbs and body, dismissing sight and hearing, departing from bodily form and abandoning knowledge, becoming one with the Great Pervader"—that is, thoroughly relinquishing the discriminating constructions of both body and mind, becoming one, undivided, with the Way itself—the highest state of spiritual freedom attainable within the Daoist framework of self-cultivation.

Though the doctrines of "fasting the mind" and "sitting in forgetfulness" first emerged as early as the mid-Warring States period, their spiritual orientation of "emptiness, quiescence, few desires; forgetting both self and world" exerted an influence, impossible to overstate, on both later Buddhist Chan (particularly during the period of "matching concepts," when Prajñāpāramitā emptiness-concepts were rendered through the vocabulary of Laozi and Zhuangzi) and on the theoretical traditions of Daoist breathing-cultivation and internal alchemy. Scholars generally hold that a considerable degree of mutual, bidirectional interpenetration and borrowing of discourse occurred between the two traditions—though the precise historical pathways of this exchange remain a complex subject within the study of Buddhist-Daoist interaction, one beyond the scope of full treatment here.

### 2. The Quiet-Sitting Tradition in Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism: From "Emphasizing Quiescence" to "Emphasizing Reverent Attentiveness" to the "Jiangmen Learning"

The tradition of cultivational discipline among the great masters of Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism (Daoxue) likewise forms a strand—closely interwoven with, yet independent of, Buddhist contemplative practice—of "quiet contemplation" spanning several centuries. Zhou Dunyi (周敦頤, 1017–1073) of the Northern Song, in his *Taijitu Shuo* (太極圖說, "Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate"), proposed that "the sage settles these [the Way of Heaven, Earth, and Human] through centrality, rightness, benevolence, and righteousness, emphasizing quiescence, and thereby establishes the ultimate standard for humanity"—later summarized as the doctrine of "emphasizing quiescence to establish the ultimate standard for humanity" (主靜立人極), the founding statement of Neo-Confucian quiet-sitting cultivational theory. Yet the Cheng brothers (Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi), although they had studied under Zhou Dunyi, turned in their own philosophical system toward "principle" (li, 天理) as the core ontological category, and their corresponding cultivational discipline shifted accordingly from "emphasizing quiescence" to "emphasizing reverent attentiveness" (主敬)—that is, apprehending the substantial and unwavering principle of Heaven through the sustained maintenance of a psychological state of "reverent attentiveness" (focus, gravity, and freedom from laxity), rather than focusing on "quiet sitting" as a particular bodily posture and form of practice in its own right. This shift "from emphasizing quiescence to emphasizing reverent attentiveness" in itself reflects a profound divergence of opinion within Neo-Confucianism over whether quiet sitting ought to be given special emphasis at all.

By the time of Zhu Xi in the Southern Song, an extensive commentary had been produced on Zhou Dunyi's doctrine of "emphasizing quiescence to establish the ultimate standard for humanity," analyzing "centrality, rightness, benevolence, and righteousness" into the two aspects of "yang in motion" and "yin in quiescence," with "quiescence ever serving as the ruling principle"—still working, at the level of doctrinal exegesis, to extend and deepen the theoretical lineage of "emphasizing quiescence." The figure who truly developed "quiet sitting" into a concrete, practicable technique of cultivation—one explicitly regarded by his own later followers as a "gateway into the Way"—was Chen Xianzhang (陳獻章, 1428–1500, styled Baisha, a native of Xinhui in Guangdong, founder of the Jiangmen school) of the Ming dynasty: his fundamental teaching held that one should "nurture the sprouts [of moral feeling] from within quiet sitting" (於靜坐中養出端倪)—cultivating and clarifying the mind through quiet sitting so as to apprehend and nourish the natural manifestation of the innate moral mind (its "sprouts"). This teaching was subsequently revised and developed by his disciple Zhan Ruoshui (湛若水, 1466–1560, styled Ganquan) into the doctrine of "apprehending the Principle of Heaven wherever one may be" (隨處體認天理)—Zhan Ruoshui held explicitly that the Principle of Heaven need not be apprehended solely through "quietly sitting to clarify the mind"; whether through quiet sitting or through engagement with worldly affairs, the Principle of Heaven could be apprehended anywhere. The Baisha school of mind-learning, the Ganquan school of mind-learning, and the subsequently ascendant Yangming school of mind-learning together constitute the main body of the Ming-dynasty intellectual current known as "the learning of the mind" (xinxue). Wang Ji, a disciple of Wang Yangming, once summarized his master's own teaching as encompassing three distinct paths to awakening: "awakening attained through intellectual understanding is called 'analytical awakening'; awakening attained through quiet sitting is called 'verificational awakening'; and awakening attained through practical engagement with worldly affairs... is called 'thorough awakening'"—and he classified Chen Baisha's practice of emphasizing quiescence under the category of "verificational awakening," rather than the "thorough awakening" that Wang Yangming himself held in higher regard. Though this assessment, offered from the standpoint of a later follower of Yangming, may not be entirely impartial, it nonetheless clearly delineates the historical position—and the controversy surrounding that position—that "quiet sitting" occupied as a form of cultivational discipline within Song-Ming Neo-Confucianism: it was, on the one hand, a genuinely significant strand within the broader genealogy of Neo-Confucian cultivational theory, persisting across several centuries; yet, on the other hand, it never achieved the kind of independent, school-founding status that Tiantai's "threefold contemplation within a single mind" or Sengchou's "four foundations of mindfulness" achieved within Buddhism, but remained instead subordinate to, and in service of, Neo-Confucianism's own more fundamental Confucian ethical concern: "preserving the Principle of Heaven, recovering the original mind."

## Part Fifteen: A Modern Derivative—The Multiple Origins of the Secularized "Mindfulness" Movement

Since the latter half of the twentieth century, the concept of "mindfulness"—and the associated training methods derived from Buddhist contemplative tradition, yet substantially transformed through a process of "de-religionization" and "clinicalization"—have achieved unprecedented, worldwide diffusion across secular domains including medicine, psychotherapy, education, corporate management, and even athletic training. This constitutes the most conspicuous contemporary derivative phenomenon of "Chan beyond Huineng's Southern School" in the present day, and merits inclusion as the concluding subject of this study.

The most representative founding figure of this movement is Jon Kabat-Zinn (b. 1944), trained originally in molecular biology. In 1979 he founded the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, developing the eight-week "Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction" (MBSR) course, which combines seated meditation, the body scan, mindful stretching, and hatha yoga. In 1990 he published *Full Catastrophe Living*; MBSR courses have since expanded to more than 700 hospitals, clinics, and research institutions worldwide, exerting a profound influence on psychosomatic medicine, clinical psychology, corporate training, and numerous other fields—in 2014, *Time* magazine ran a feature story under the title "The Mindful Revolution."

It is particularly worth clarifying that Kabat-Zinn's own roots in practice are, in fact, considerably more diverse, and cannot simply be reduced to a single line of inheritance from the Theravāda insight-meditation tradition: according to his own accounts and related biographical material, he studied in his early years under teachers within the Zen tradition, including Philip Kapleau, Thich Nhat Hanh, and Seung Sahn—of whom Thich Nhat Hanh belongs to the Vietnamese Lâm Tế (Linji) lineage (the Liễu Quán and Trúc Lâm traditions, too, were profoundly shaped by Chinese Chan) and Seung Sahn to the Korean Jogye Order—both of which, strictly speaking, belong to the historical eastward extension of "Huineng's lineage" itself into East Asia, and are thus part of the Southern School Chan system proper, not instances of the "beyond Huineng" tradition that is this study's subject. Kabat-Zinn was also a founding member of the Cambridge Zen Center, and his own personal practice further encompasses hatha yoga, Theravāda vipassanā insight meditation, and the teachings of the Sōtō Zen school and Advaita Vedanta. In other words, the actual intellectual roots of MBSR are the product of Kabat-Zinn's own personal synthesis of multiple traditions—Southern-School Chan, Theravāda insight meditation, and yoga among them—rather than the far simpler genealogical narrative commonly assumed in both scholarly and popular media accounts, that "MBSR equals a secularized form of Theravāda insight meditation." This clarification itself echoes the methodological caution that has run throughout this entire study: any oversimplified, single-lineage narrative of "the genealogy of Chan traditions" risks obscuring the intricate, multi-source confluence that actually characterizes their historical formation.

## Conclusion: The Genealogical Landscape of the Various Meditation Traditions, and Methodological Reflections

Drawing together the findings of the fifteen parts above, the traditions surveyed under the heading "Chan or meditation methods beyond Huineng's Southern School" may broadly be grouped into the following several genealogical strands—strands that are at once relatively independent of one another and yet deeply interwoven and interpenetrating:

**First, branches internal to the Bodhidharma lineage that nonetheless failed to enter the orthodox "Huineng—Five Houses and Seven Schools" narrative.** This is the newly emphasized focus of this study following the mid-course correction to its scope: the original teachings of the five shared patriarchs—Bodhidharma, Huike, Sengcan, Daoxin, and Hongren—themselves (the Two Entrances and Four Practices, wall-contemplation, the East Mountain Teaching's doctrine of "guarding the mind"), together with the philological problem of "layered accretion" in the source material; the Sengchou lineage's four-foundations-of-mindfulness meditation, whose standing in the Northern Dynasties at one point eclipsed that of the Bodhidharma–Huike lineage; the gradual-cultivation meditation teaching of Shenxiu's Northern School, "Dharma-Master of the Two Capitals, Teacher to Three Emperors" in the High Tang; the independently developing Prajñā-based Chan of the Oxhead School, which would later resonate strikingly with the Southern School's own sudden-awakening doctrine; and the Jingzhong and Baotang schools of Sichuan, which formed a three-way rivalry with the Northern and Southern Schools and spread still farther, into Tibet. Together, these traditions reveal a core historical fact: the now-familiar single-line genealogy of Chan, with its "special transmission outside the teachings, mind-to-mind transmission," is the product of a prolonged contest over historical narrative-construction, waged by Shenhui and his successors—it is not the natural, sole possible course that Chan's development had to take. At numerous specific junctures in this history, "orthodoxy" and "mainstream status" simply failed to coincide; the power of contingent factors—imperial patronage, geographical and institutional resources, the vigor of one's later disciples—to shape the historical record has been no less significant than the relative depth or sophistication of doctrine itself.

**Second, independent Chinese traditions that share a common origin with, yet diverged from, the various branches of the Bodhidharma lineage, tracing their doctrinal roots to different bodies of Indian scripture and treatise literature.** This includes the An Shigao–Kumārajīva lineage of translated meditation sūtras, already systematically established before Bodhidharma's arrival in China; the Tiantai zhiguan system, developing independently with doctrinal roots tracing directly to Nāgārjunian Madhyamaka; the Pure Land buddha-recollection samādhi tradition, traceable to the *Pratyutpanna Samādhi Sūtra*; the five-fold vijñaptimātratā contemplation, grounded in the *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra*; and the Huayan Dharmadhātu contemplation (the four Dharmadhātus), grounded in the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s "dependent origination of the Dharmadhātu" and progressively completed from Dushun through Chengguan. All of these traditions arose and circulated within the Chinese-language Buddhist cultural sphere, sharing the basic doctrinal framework of Mahāyāna Buddhism with the various branches of the Bodhidharma lineage, yet each developing independently on the basis of its own distinct scriptural foundations, lineages of transmission, and techniques of practice and realization.

**Third, Esoteric Buddhist traditions sharing a common origin in Indian Mahāyāna–Vajrayāna Buddhism, yet developing independently along different geographical pathways.** Chinese Tang Esotericism (the correspondence of the three mysteries, maṇḍala visualization) and Tibetan Esoteric Buddhism (Mahāmudrā, Dzogchen), though both tracing back to late Indian Esoteric origins, historically had little in the way of direct contact, each developing, within China proper and on the Tibetan plateau respectively, highly refined and internally coherent systems of profound contemplative practice and realization—and Tibet's own history of Esoteric contemplative practice, moreover, through Moheyan's "Debate of Sudden and Gradual" and the earlier transmission of the Sichuan lineages, came into direct historical contact with the very "beyond Huineng" traditions described in Part Six of this study.

**Fourth, the Theravāda śamatha-vipassanā tradition, sharing a common Indian origin with the various branches of the Bodhidharma lineage yet preserved independently, intact, since the schism of the early Buddhist schools, down to the present day.** Its framework of "the four jhānas and eight samādhis" is the very origin of what "Chan" originally signified; since the twentieth century, through the modernizing reforms of a series of Burmese meditation masters—Ledi Sayadaw, Mahasi Sayadaw, U Ba Khin, Goenka, and others—it has further given rise to an insight-meditation movement open to lay practitioners the world over, which, together with the MBSR movement founded by Kabat-Zinn, now together constitute the most widely recognized contemporary derivative forms of "Chan" practice in secular society.

**Fifth, indigenous Chinese traditions of quiet contemplation that, while not Buddhist, interacted deeply with Buddhist meditation over a long history.** The Daoist tradition of "fasting the mind" and "sitting in forgetfulness," and the Song-Ming Neo-Confucian tradition of quiet-sitting cultivational discipline, though falling outside the proper conceptual category of Buddhist "Chan" (*dhyāna*), nonetheless underwent profound bidirectional interpenetration with Buddhist contemplative practice over the specific course of Chinese intellectual history, providing an essential frame of reference for understanding the full complexity of what "Chan" has come to mean within the Chinese-language cultural context.

A final point bears re-emphasis regarding the methodological stance this study has maintained throughout: the reason these various traditions have, for so long, been overshadowed in popular understanding—and even within a portion of internal Buddhist discourse—by the later-arising yet extraordinarily influential narrative of "Huineng's Southern School," is, to a considerable degree, itself a product of historical narrative-construction: the transmission-record literature's rhetorical strategy of "a special transmission outside the teachings, mind-to-mind transmission," the sustained polemical efforts of Shenhui and his successors, and the overwhelming social and cultural dominance the Southern School itself achieved from the Song dynasty onward, together shaped a popular understanding that treats "Chan" as all but synonymous with "the Southern School of the Chan sect." The twentieth-century discovery of the Dunhuang manuscripts, the excavation of archaeological materials such as the Xiaonanhai caves, and the sustained philological and historical scholarship of successive generations of scholars—from Hu Shi and Yinshun to Yanagida Seizan and, more recently, Eric Greene—represent precisely the ongoing effort to break through this popular understanding, and gradually to restore "Chan" to its true original character: a category of religious practice far more ancient, and far more plural, than "the Southern School" alone. The writing of this study is itself an attempt at a systematic synthesis undertaken along the continuing trajectory of that scholarly effort.
